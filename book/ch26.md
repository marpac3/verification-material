# 26. The Road Ahead

Verification's limiting constraint is choosing what to check, as the next generation of the book's DMA architecture illustrates when it returns to tape-out. The flagship SoC's DMA has eight channels and inherits the reference SoC's register frontend, burst splitter and AXI backend, together with its discipline of splitting bursts at the 4 KB boundary, with 40-bit physical addressing and descriptor chaining. The register frontend is the DMA's first stage, the register interface that software programs. A DMA channel is an independent transfer engine with a state of its own and a share of the AXI backend. The burst splitter is the DMA stage that splits each transfer into bursts that the AXI address rules allow, and the 4 KB boundary is the address line that AXI forbids a burst to cross. The 4 KB boundary is now enforced by a formal property rather than only by a scoreboard check that depends on receiving the right transfer descriptor, the record that software writes to program one DMA transfer. The stimulus is a scenario space described once and retargeted from simulation to the prototype, rather than a constraint block written for one environment; §26.2 names that practice. Regressions gate on every commit and coverage merges across platforms; machine time is no longer disputed by this block's team.

The bug that arrives in week 31 is SOC-1284's descendant. The burst splitter again mis-splits a negative-stride 2D row across the 4 KB boundary: wider addresses and a different module retain the arithmetic mistake, but the second half now writes a line the host cluster holds Modified. The host cluster is the flagship's cluster of application-class cores. A 2D row is a single line of a two-dimensional transfer, a contiguous run of bytes, and the stride is the signed address step from the start of one row to the start of the next. A cache holds a line Modified when it has written to that line and has not yet returned it to memory. The scoreboard that caught the original sees nothing, because the corrupted bytes never reach memory during the check; they surface at a writeback, later, somewhere else. The team finds it eventually, from a firmware symptom on the prototype: weeks of debug on a platform where the corrupted line is not visible, for a bug whose ancestor a scoreboard caught in one night.

In SOC-1284, two hundred and fourteen directed tests passed and a constrained-random run reached the combination: a negative stride, a crossing of the 4 KB boundary and a second DMA channel active at the same time. A directed test is a piece of stimulus whose content and order are written out by hand, and constrained-random is the automatic generation of legal but unlikely stimulus under constraints. Escape analysis recorded that the functional coverage model lacked a cross of those three dimensions, leaving the hole invisible to the team's metrics; a cross is the coverage item that counts the runs in which two or more conditions hold together. Escape analysis is the blameless structured post-mortem of an escape. A coverage model is the multi-dimensional region that attributes and their values mark out, and an attribute is a single dimension of a behavior together with the set of values it can take. In the descendant, no engine was wrong: the sound proof covered the *previous* generation's backend but entered the new plan as a plan row already discharged. A plan row is the line of the verification plan that carries one feature, its method and the evidence that closes it, and it is discharged when that evidence exists and is linked to it. No plan row stated that a data-integrity check crossing into a coherent domain is a different claim requiring different evidence. Both outcomes were constrained by knowing what to ask, rather than by compute, stimulus or checking.

### Chapter map

§26.1 traces the constraint's migration across four generations of tooling, the evidence for each stage and its dependence on verification level. §26.2 examines the benefits and limits of shift-left and shift-right and their combined pressure on the middle of the flow. §26.3 distinguishes properties that survive better tooling from those that only seem permanent. §26.4 describes verification as a profession and four weaknesses it retains.

## 26.1 The bottleneck moves

This section's historical argument is the book's interpretation, not a conclusion of the cited studies. The sources supply the evidence for each stage, and each measurement belongs to that stage: figures taken in different years, from different populations and with different instruments cannot form a trend line, just as two speed-ups measured against different baselines cannot be multiplied. The stages share a pattern without a common quantitative scale.

A binding constraint is the activity to which a doubled budget would go. Verification's binding constraint has occupied four positions in turn.

**Cycles.** The founding constraint was raw simulation throughput. RTL simulation runs approximately a billion times slower than the target clock at full application-class scale, the high end of the band this book declares, and that ratio is why an operating-system boot takes years in a simulator for seconds of silicon execution; it is also why hardware-assisted platforms exist to recover a hundred to a thousand times of that slow-down [cit:P21] (Chapter 17 works the economics). This constraint has not disappeared; it has been *relocated by level*. For a block environment the size of the reference SoC's DMA, cycles have long ceased to bind because the nightly suite fits in a night. They still bind firmware runs on the flagship: the 2024 industry data records that plateauing processor frequency scaling leaves simulation-based techniques struggling with growing complexity, making acceleration essential for many designs [cit:R2].

**Stimulus.** Affordable cycles made stimulus selection the next constraint. Hand-enumerated tests stop scaling in the low hundreds (Chapter 9); replacing enumeration with description lets a constraint solver generate legal but unlikely traffic by the thousand, an approach that arrived with the constraint-based verification languages of the late 1990s and became the industry default. The reported limits identify the level at which stimulus generation ceased to bind. In 2014, constrained-random adoption appeared to be leveling off, with scaling offered as the reason: the technique works well at IP block or subsystem level but does not scale to entire SoC integration [cit:P17]. The difficulty at that level is deciding *what* to generate rather than generating stimulus. A decade later, constrained-random simulation was the one exception to general stabilization of simulation-technique adoption [cit:R2]; that continued growth is consistent with a technique that has solved its problem and is still reaching teams that have not used it.

**Checking.** A testbench must evaluate the traffic it generates. The self-checking discipline addressed this constraint by computing the verdict rather than inspecting waveforms (Chapter 3), architecting the environment so that stimulus, driving, monitoring, checking and coverage are five separable responsibilities (Chapter 8), and using assertions to carry rules local enough to state (Chapter 11). The adoption data shows this in standardization: SystemVerilog Assertions is the predominant assertion language and UVM the predominant IC/ASIC testbench methodology [cit:R2]. The video codec illustrates the remaining limits. A standards body that publishes a bit-exact reference decoder supplies the oracle: expected pixels are defined independently of opinion, and checking reduces to a comparison. The standard leaves rate control and latency unspecified, along with encoder behavior under line-buffer back-pressure; someone must decide to write those checkers. Chapter 3 derives the rule from this case and the instruction-set simulator: a definitional oracle defines exactly one projection of correctness.

**Knowing what to check.** Choosing what to check now binds; the evidence is necessarily indirect because unasked questions leave no trace in tool output.

First-silicon success is the outcome in which the first fabricated parts are already fit for production. First-silicon success stood at 14 percent in 2024, the lowest value recorded in twenty years and the low point of a decline since 2012 [cit:R2]. A decade earlier it was about 30 percent [cit:P17]. On the FPGA side, 87 percent of projects reported non-trivial bugs escaping into production in 2024 [cit:R1]; that side is a different population measured by a different metric, not a point on the IC/ASIC curve. The two figures need not and cannot combine into one. Improved engines and wider adoption of mature techniques coexist with poor outcomes on both metrics; these observations do not establish that better engines alone improve outcomes.

The 2024 report traces complexity growth to new requirement layers beyond basic functionality: clocking, security, safety and hardware-software interaction requirements that did not exist years ago [cit:R2]. Each layer introduces questions that must be formulated before an engine can answer them. Design errors top the root-cause data; changing, incorrect or incomplete specifications remain a common concern for verification engineers and project managers [cit:R2]. A specification defect directs checking at the wrong claim.

The 2024 study reads the spread in verification time as a reuse effect: projects spending the least time typically reuse pre-verified IP, while those spending the most carry high proportions of newly developed IP [cit:R2]. Reused IP can cost less because someone has already worked out what to check, without its logic being simpler.

Three further signals concern the work of defining and interpreting checks. Debug, the human work of understanding a failure, took more of a verification engineer's time than any other activity in 2024 and is named the discipline's major bottleneck [cit:R2]. Automatic formal applications were the fastest-growing formal category in the 2024 data, growing at an 8.7 percent compound annual rate against 5.8 percent for property checking [cit:R2]; such an application supplies its own properties from a register description or a connectivity table, so for those narrow classes the work of deciding what to check moves upstream, to whoever wrote that description or that table (Chapter 15). A property set cannot establish whether it is sufficient, and Bormann's completeness criterion supplies the test from outside it. The criterion asks whether the properties allow unique determination of one output trace for each input trace; on a published project list, almost all the circuits verified to that criterion functioned properly after fabrication, and the few overlooked faults were traced to misapplication of the methodology rather than to the criterion [cit:T2] (Chapter 14 states its refinement in full). This implemented method is expensive, and what it tests specifically is the sufficiency of the property set, which no other part of the toolchain can assess.

The reference SoC illustrates the same constraint at block scale. A beat is a single data transfer within a burst. The neural accelerator's weight prefetcher wraps its buffer pointer one entry early when the weight stream ends mid-beat under DMA contention. It escaped 100 percent code coverage entirely: the line was executed and the branch was taken, but nothing about the structural metric could represent the conjunction. Only a functional cross of weight precision × tensor width class × contention demands the scenario, and that cross exists only if somebody decided those were the three axes; no engine makes that choice.

Career specialization should follow the constraint at the relevant level. At block level, the model of what must be true should be assessed alongside throughput; on a full-chip firmware workload, cycles may still be the constraint. Both require the skill of translating specifications into claims precise enough for a machine to check.

## 26.2 Shift-left and shift-right

**Shift-left** means verification starting earlier and running against something other than finished RTL. Static analysis moves structural questions to the earliest point at which they can be asked: clock-domain crossing tools identify domain issues directly on an RTL model at earlier stages of the design flow because the class of metastability bug they target cannot be demonstrated on an RTL model in simulation at all and is hard to reproduce in the lab [cit:R2] (Chapter 13). Virtual platforms let firmware run against functional models of the memory map before the hardware exists, offering far more controllability and observability than debugging the same code over a debug port, and sometimes being the only option available while the hardware is still under development [cit:T1] (Chapter 18). Controllability is the ability to steer a design into a given internal condition using only drivable interfaces. Portable stimulus describes a scenario space once and retargets it across platforms, so the modelling work precedes any particular environment (Chapter 9). Continuous integration binds the whole loop to the commit rather than to the milestone; one 2026 research-programme account reports that running UVM-based verification and FPGA-based checks inside CI increased productivity by making the verification flow transparent from RTL development [cit:P25].

Shift-left's specific benefit is finding defects in the *specification* earlier, beyond advancing bug-finding. Writing an assertion against a document rather than a design can expose a difference between its text and the assumed meaning; Chapter 11 recommends this as a low-cost way to detect specification ambiguity. This addresses the persistent specification problems in the root-cause picture of §26.1.

The sensor hub illustrates both the architectural work and its limits. The sensor hub's always-on 32 kHz island, wake sources and retention list are architectural decisions made and reviewed in this example long before RTL implementation. The design's power intent, in the form standardized by IEEE Std 1801-2024 (UPF), is which domains exist, what retains and which supply-state combinations are legal [cit:S10]. Power intent is the description held outside the design source, because the same logic is integrated into different power architectures; UPF, the unified power format, is that description in machine-readable form. This early architectural work does not simulate a design. This architectural work cannot reach the reference SoC's canonical escape in its event unit, because that escape is a property of the implementation's sequencing. In that bug a retention register loses its configuration when a wake event races the release of isolation, and the bug surfaced in power-aware gate-level simulation after RTL sign-off (Chapter 19). Isolation is the defined behavior of a signal while its driving logic is unpowered, and its release is the moment that behavior ends. Shift-left moves *claims* earlier without moving their evidence; confusing them can leave a project at week 40 with an early plan and no discharged rows.

**Shift-right** means the design continues to be checked after tape-out. **Validation** is checking real hardware rather than a model, the distinction Chapter 20 states in full; it is a planned stage with its own instruments rather than a fallback for what verification missed; and the industry commits silicon to that stage: in some modern SoCs over 20 percent of the design real estate, alongside a significant component of the CAD-flow effort, is devoted to silicon validation [cit:P21]. The flagship carries per-core processor trace, eight bus monitors with programmable triggers, a 64 KB on-chip trace buffer and a debug access port per power domain.

Shift-right covers what no pre-silicon platform provides: the real device running real software at real speed for real durations. It cannot supply observability beyond the hardware capacity. Every mechanism writes into a fixed store, so **signals × cycles ≤ buffer**: 64 KB buys 128 traced signals for 4,096 cycles, or 256 signals for 2,048, and that rectangle of signals against cycles is fixed at tape-out (Chapter 20). Lab work cannot enlarge that store, so post-silicon debug must deliver a pre-silicon reproducer, short enough to simulate and small enough to load while still failing; an explanation alone does not meet that requirement. Shift-right should therefore return problems to the middle of the flow.

The book's judgment is that these directions put the middle of the flow under strain. The block-level RTL environment, covered in this book's middle parts, is the only stage with both full observability and a real design. Shift-left puts pressure on block-level RTL verification by moving claims onto models that cannot carry the evidence. Shift-right adds pressure by promising that whatever remains will be caught on a platform that no longer offers full observability, and the cost of finding anything there rises for that reason. Both movements are correct in their own terms. Pursuing both can leave a strong early plan and lab with little block-level verification between them, despite the full observability and real design available at that stage.

> **Scenario.** Late in the reference SoC project, the SoC integration team proposes cutting the crossbar's block-level constrained-random regression by half, on the grounds that its protocol properties are formally proven and its integration behaviour will be covered in the lab. **Approach.** The verification lead accepts the first argument and refuses the second: proven properties retire the protocol rows, so those runs are removed. The remaining plan rows concern *arbitration under sustained back-pressure across two managers*, which is proven only up to a bound and reachable in the lab but not diagnosable within its trace buffer, so those seeds stay. A bounded proof is a result stating only that no violation exists within a stated number of cycles from reset. **Why.** The two ends of the flow retired one class of evidence and neither of them retired the other. Only a retirement argument for each row identifies which half was kept; a percentage cut cannot do so.

## 26.3 What does not change

A single test distinguishes properties of the problem from tooling limits: whether each would remain true if every tool became a thousand times better. Confusing these categories leaves readers ten years later unable to identify which half of an account has become obsolete.

Six properties meet this test.

**State-space size grows exponentially with the number of state bits, making abstraction necessary.** A state space is the set of all the configurations a design's state elements can take. One DMA channel's 192 bits of architecturally visible state yield 2<sup>192</sup>, about 10<sup>57</sup>, configurations; simulation visits a vanishing fraction (Chapter 3 gives the arithmetic). A thousandfold faster engine adds capacity for ten state bits. In the coherent hub, the MESI state of every line in every cache forms a product space no tool can enumerate, so the proof runs against a model cut down to two caches and one address, small enough to converge and large enough to contain the livelock and starvation cases that motivate proving coherence. Abstraction remains necessary even with a thousand times more capacity because the space grows faster than any engine.

**Simulation shows the presence of bugs, never their absence.** Testing yields probabilistic assurance, not proof (Chapter 3). This is a statement about sampling, and no improvement in sampling rate converts a sample into a census.

**Every practical oracle is partial.** Reference models, scoreboards and assertions each check a projection of correctness; they must be combined so their blind spots do not overlap (Chapter 3). A partial oracle is a checker that verifies one projection of correctness rather than correctness itself. The radar front-end illustrates this within one design. Its FFT and detection pipelines use bit-exact arithmetic and therefore an equality oracle. One stage upstream, the receive chain from mixer through variable-gain amplifier to ADC needs a band, a window and a condition set for the same signal, and every downstream checker, coverage measure and pass/fail decision is rebuilt on those three (Chapter 21). The band is the tolerance the measured signal must stay inside, the window is the time interval over which it is measured, and the condition set is the set of operating conditions under which the band holds. An engine cannot convert that band into an equality, and someone must choose the band as part of the specification, whether or not the decision is recorded there.

**A proof is a statement about a model, under assumptions, sometimes to a depth.** "Formally verified" must state those three qualifiers as applicable: the model and assumptions, and any depth bound (Chapter 14). These enduring qualifications differ from the often-stated claim that "formal doesn't scale," which describes tooling and will go out of date. The qualifications remain: a model always abstracts something, assumptions always make claims about an environment, and a bound always limits the proof.

**A plan specifies claims, and coverage samples an authored model.** An environment has two separate aspects here: it records that a situation occurred, and it judges whether the design behaved correctly in it. The two draw on the same monitors, and their fidelities are traded off against one another [cit:B5]. Coverage therefore measures sampling rather than correctness (Chapter 6): "100 percent" describes the model's extent without establishing its fidelity. This explains SOC-1284's and the accelerator's blind spots and survives the automation of Chapter 25: judgment still shapes a drafted plan's claims, and the plan cannot detect what it cannot represent.

**Activation, propagation and checking are three independent conditions, and controllability and observability bound the first two.** A bug found requires all three: stimulus must activate it, the wrong value must propagate to a point the environment can see, and a check there must recognize the value as wrong. Each of the three can fail alone (Chapter 3). Observability is therefore designed into the hardware a year before it is needed, with a fixed capacity shared between signals and cycles rather than an adjustable observation range.

Three further claims describe current tools and practice. *Debug dominates verification time* was measured in 2014 and again in 2024 [cit:P17,R2]. The finding is a robust empirical regularity that describes current practice, and it is the most plausible candidate here for automation to change. Such a change would move the fourth of the constraints of §26.1, knowing what to check, rather than remove that constraint, since explaining a failure in seconds does not decide which questions to ask. *Formal is capacity-limited to small blocks* is a statement about engines. *Simulation is a billion times slower than silicon* measures today's tools rather than a permanent property. One further unsupported claim is that *70 percent of a project's overall effort is spent in verification*. Harry Foster is the author of the study series that supplies most of the measured numbers here. He identifies that figure as an example of the field's subjective and unsubstantiated claims; he points instead to a measured lineage: the Collett International Research studies of 2002 and 2004, the later of which had 201 eligible participants, and then the Wilson Research Group studies, with 1,886 eligible participants in 2014 [cit:P17]. The series applies that standard to itself: 1,492 eligible participants in the 2020 study, 980 in the 2022 one [cit:R3,R5]. A reported figure should include its sample size.

## 26.4 Verification as a profession

Chapter 1's first pitfall is treating verification as a phase that starts when the RTL is done. Four independent observations establish verification as a profession.

**Headcount.** Demand for verification engineers has outgrown demand for design engineers since at least 2007: verification demand grew at a 12.5 percent compound annual rate through 2014 and design demand at 3.7 percent, and by that year the mean peak verification headcount per project already exceeded the design count [cit:P17]. The growth rates have since converged, starting from that inversion of the two headcounts, and in some market segments, including processors, a five-to-one ratio of verification to design engineers is not unusual [cit:R2].

**Boundary.** Organizational divisions no longer distinguish the work. In 2024, design engineers spent on average 49 percent of their time on verification activities [cit:R2]. Nearly half of design headcount thus represents verification effort, defining the profession by activity rather than organization. Chapter 24 takes up how such teams are built and how verifiers are trained.

**Apparatus.** The discipline has its own standards: an assertion language (IEEE Std 1800-2023) [cit:S8], a testbench methodology (IEEE Std 1800.2-2020) [cit:S9], a stimulus description language (Accellera PSS 3.0) [cit:S1], a coverage interchange format (Accellera UCIS 1.0) [cit:S3], a power-intent format (IEEE Std 1801-2024) [cit:S10], and as of March 2026 a standard for describing an IP's clock- and reset-domain-crossing interface so that different teams' tools can exchange collateral about it [cit:S12]. These standards identify the work of making claims portable between people rather than accelerating simulation. The newest standard's Accellera working group counted 154 members from 24 companies as of September 2025 [cit:D36]; the standard lists Agnisys, Arm, Blue Pearl Solutions, Google, IBM, Infineon, Intel, Marvell, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Renesas, Siemens EDA, STMicroelectronics and Synopsys as eligible voting companies at the time of standardization [cit:S12]. Three years of collaboration among competitors indicates the importance they assigned to this problem.

**Theory.** The discipline has its own reasoning about state-space arguments and the oracle problem, coverage as sampling, and proofs qualified by model, assumptions and depth; these principles should inform daily engineering even when the engineer cannot name them.

Judgment is the professional contribution that tooling has been least able to absorb through four generations. Reasoning about what could be true rather than what is makes the work demanding, suiting some minds and frustrating others. The work also carries an unusual professional obligation, Chapter 2's independence of judgment: a willingness to say "no, this is not the way to do it" to a designer with more years on the block, or to a schedule that wants the block closed. This is a duty even when inconvenient, independent of personality.

Four weaknesses remain in the field, in the assessment offered here. Each offers an individual an opportunity to improve on current practice.

**Escape analysis and subsequent plans.** Escape analysis should feed changes into the next plan and sign-off checklist (Chapter 7 gives the five-question form, Chapter 20 the post-silicon version), rather than end with a post-mortem slide. Using the next plan and sign-off checklist to carry those findings into the next project provides an existing mechanism for accumulating organizational knowledge.

**Effort estimation remains unresolved.** Debug takes more of a verification engineer's time than any other activity, and it is unpredictable and varies significantly between projects; those findings together make one thing a stated, unresolved management problem: estimates of effort and schedule drawn from the previous project's data [cit:R2]. Such estimates should account for those limits. Three quarters of IC/ASIC projects were behind their original schedule in 2024, against a historical average of about two thirds [cit:R2].

**Measurement of verification environments.** Measurement of the design's behavior and measurement of a verification environment address different questions: the latter should establish whether its checks can still detect a change. Mutation tests (Chapter 14) are how that capability is demonstrated: each injects a deliberate change into the design to check that the environment's checks detect it. A checker has no demonstrated detection ability until it has been seen to fail; this applies to a whole environment as well as a single assertion.

**Cross-domain sign-off has no common measure.** A functional sign-off aggregates coverage and plan rows. A safety argument aggregates fault classification against per-level metric thresholds (Chapter 22). A security result is a list of named claims and named gaps with no percentage available at all, and there will not be one soon (Chapter 23). These three incommensurable results reach the same tape-out review without an accepted way to state a combined position.

### In practice

Three practices address these risks. Inherited plan rows should be re-derived: a row marked *verified in the previous generation* should be reopened and its assumptions reread against the new integration, the step the flagship's DMA program skipped. An explicit written register should identify what the current program is *not* verifying, including gaps as well as waivers, because named gaps can be reviewed while unnamed ones remain surprises. A waiver is a written record that accepts a specific hole in the evidence, with its reason, its risk argument, its owner and its expiry. A fraction of every program should fund block-level environments on the newest logic despite competing demands from early modeling and a well-equipped lab. Where the three practices lack an associated metric, budget reviews should make the contribution of all three explicit.

### Pitfalls

1. **Reading the bottleneck as industry-wide.** *Symptom:* a methodology decision justified by "the industry has moved past simulation throughput" on a block whose regression does not fit in a night. *Cure:* the constraint should be identified per level and per block before any reallocation.
2. **Treating shift-left as evidence rather than as claims arriving early.** *Symptom:* a plan whose rows were written in month two and whose closure column is still empty in month nine. *Cure:* every early row should specify a platform and a date for producing evidence; otherwise closure remains unscheduled.
3. **Reduced block-level verification between the two ends.** *Symptom:* strong architectural modeling and a well-equipped lab coexist with block environments staffed by whoever is free. *Cure:* middle-of-the-flow work should be cut only row by row with a retirement argument, never by percentage.
4. **Confusing a durable property with a tooling limit.** *Symptom:* a design rule of thumb inherited from a capacity constraint that lifted two tool generations ago, still shaping how blocks are partitioned. *Cure:* under the thousandfold test, a rule changed by a better engine is a budget decision that belongs in the plan for reconsideration.
5. **Skilling only at the ends.** *Symptom:* a team fluent in architectural models and lab bring-up that cannot debug a scoreboard mismatch at RTL. Bring-up is the phase in which the environment and the DUT first exchange checked transactions. *Cure:* both skills ultimately depend on block-level verification; a pre-silicon reproducer is useless if the team cannot run it.

### Summary

- Verification's binding constraint has moved from cycles to stimulus to checking to knowing what to check; each tooling generation resolved a constraint and exposed the next. The migration is per-level, not uniform: cycles still bind full-chip firmware runs.
- The measured outcomes limit what can be claimed for better engines. IC/ASIC first-silicon success fell to 14 percent in 2024 from about 30 percent a decade earlier [cit:R2,P17]; separately, 87 percent of FPGA projects reported non-trivial bugs escaping into production in 2024 [cit:R1]. Adoption of the mature techniques climbed and then saturated across the same span. These outcomes do not establish that better engines alone improve results.
- Shift-left buys specification defects found early; shift-right buys the real device at real speed. Their contributions are distinct; pursuing both without sustaining block-level verification leaves early claims and later debug costs with insufficient evidence between.
- What survives any improvement in tooling: exponential state spaces and abstraction as the reply; presence but never absence; partial oracles; proofs qualified by model, assumptions and depth; a plan as a set of claims and coverage as a sample of an authored model; and activation, propagation and checking as three separate conditions.
- Debug's share of effort, formal's capacity limits and the simulation-to-silicon speed ratio should not be taught as permanent properties. All three describe a practice and its tools, not the problem.
- Verification has the headcount, standards and theory of a profession; its activities also took 49 percent of design engineers' time in 2024 [cit:R2]. In the book's assessment its open problems are escape analysis that does not reach the next plan, unresolved estimation, environments that go unmeasured, and the lack of a common language for combining a functional, a safety and a security position at one review.

**From the corpus.** The two short 2024 Wilson Research Group trend reports, IC/ASIC [cit:R2] and FPGA [cit:R1], provide the field's survey record and are the most recent of the three editions held; Foster's DAC 2015 paper [cit:P17] supplies the decade-earlier baseline and the series methodology. The two editions before that pair, from 2020 and 2022, are IC/ASIC [cit:R3,R5] and FPGA [cit:R4,R6], and reading two editions together shows the movement a single edition can only assert. The requirement-layer framing and adoption curves merit attention alongside the headline percentages. Bormann's dissertation [cit:T2] addresses whether a property set is sufficient and warrants study even without adopting the methodology. Mishra et al. [cit:P21] describes changes in bug cost and visibility as a project moves right. The 2026 agentic-EDA survey [cit:P23] is a published projection of where automation is going and should be read as its authors' expectation rather than as a finding: it proposes an autonomy taxonomy modeled on the levels used for self-driving cars, offers a near-, mid- and long-term roadmap against them, notes that as of its writing no LLM-driven autonomous agent had demonstrated a complete human-free tape-out of an industrial chip, and argues that trust should move from the model that generates the artifact to a mechanically verifiable proof. In this book's interpretation, that argument makes the property somebody writes more important. The Accellera clock- and reset-domain-crossing interface standard [cit:S12], published March 2026, illustrates standardization for portability of claims between teams.

Across twenty-six chapters, verification has been treated as establishing qualified claims about designs.

Verification does not itself produce design correctness. Chapter 3 explains why the state space cannot be enumerated and every practical oracle checks a projection; Chapter 14 shows how a purpose-built completeness methodology addresses them for selected modules, at a cost. Verification establishes true claims about a design, stating the method and assumptions as well as what was not established. Sign-off states those claims and assigns named responsibility for them.

The book's instruments define and support these claims. A plan states claims in advance, while coverage records that their scope was exercised. A scoreboard can disprove a claim, and an assertion continuously checks a clause of it. A waiver states a limitation, while a proof establishes a clause without exceptions inside a named model. Escape analysis corrects a claim found to have been too broad.

SOC-1284 illustrates a false verification claim. Two hundred and fourteen directed tests passed; the dashboard was green and the coverage report accurate about everything it measured. They supported the false claim that *the DMA's splitting of 2D transfers is verified*, which no project instrument could refute. Only eleven days of margin and one constrained-random seed separated discovery in the project from discovery by a customer.

Every review, plan, dashboard and green regression should identify the exact claim and the conditions that would falsify it, so the results can be assessed as evidence for that claim.

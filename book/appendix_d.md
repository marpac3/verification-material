# Appendix D: Solutions to the exercises

Each chapter closes with four to six exercises of three kinds: a calculation whose inputs come from the
chapter or from the book's canonical numbers, a judgement between techniques that has a defensible
answer, and the reading of an artifact such as a plan row, a log excerpt, a property or a coverage
report. The solutions below give the reasoning, not only the result: the steps of a calculation with
its derivation, the argument that decides a judgement, and the section of the chapter that carries the
fact each answer rests on. Every solution was solved independently a second time before it was
accepted; where the two readings disagreed, the statement of the exercise was made unambiguous.

## Chapter 1

**Solution 1.1.** §1.2 puts the 2024 rate at 14 percent, so fourteen projects in a hundred reach production-worthy first silicon and the rest do not: 14 + 86 = 100, which makes 86 in a hundred the number that pay for at least one further fabrication cycle. The section states the same quantity as a small integer ratio, and the arithmetic that connects the two is 6 / 7 = 0.857, that is 85.7 in a hundred, which agrees with 86 to within a point; the ratio is the rounded form of the measured percentage rather than a second measurement. The rate describes IC/ASIC projects reaching first silicon in 2024. It describes no FPGA project, because an FPGA project has no metric directly comparable to a respin count, which §1.2 states where it reports the FPGA escape figure.

**Solution 1.2.** The two figures together entitle the slide to conclude that both populations fail at correctness at a high rate, and nothing about which discipline is weaker. Two properties bound the conclusion. First, the two headline percentages measure different things, one the fraction of projects whose first silicon was production-worthy and the other the fraction of projects that shipped production systems with non-trivial bugs; the FPGA paragraph of §1.2 states that FPGA projects have no metric directly comparable to a respin count, so no ordering can be read across the two. Second, the same paragraph states that the gap between the two percentages is smaller than the margin of error the studies declare, so even a comparison of like with like would not resolve at that precision. What §1.2 does support is the cultural diagnosis it gives directly, that many FPGA teams deliberately minimize pre-lab verification, a claim about practice rather than about relative discipline quality.

**Solution 1.3.** §1.3 staffs the reference SoC with four RTL designers and five dedicated verification engineers, so the project carries 4 + 5 = 9 full-time equivalents, and the same section reports that IC/ASIC design engineers spent on average 49 percent of their time on verification in 2024. Verification therefore absorbs 5 + 4 × 0.49 = 5 + 1.96 = 6.96 equivalents, that is 6.96 / 9 = 0.77, about 77 percent of the project's engineering hours. Using the section's own "near half" for the designers rather than the survey average moves the result by one point and no further: 5 + 4 × 0.5 = 7, and 7 / 9 = 0.78. Either arithmetic supports the claim §1.3 closes on, that well over half of all engineering hours go to verification on a project developing new IP. The dedicated team dominates the sum, contributing 5 of the 6.96 against the designers' 1.96, so the designers' verification time raises the figure without being what makes it exceed half.

**Solution 1.4.** §1.3 calls the 70 percent folklore, repeated from publication to publication without primary data, and puts a measured figure in its place: 57 percent of total project time on average, with wide dispersion in both directions, projects assembling pre-verified IP legitimately spending far less and projects with a high proportion of newly developed IP far more. A project assembling mostly pre-verified IP therefore belongs in the low tail and should budget below the average, not at a figure above it; the defensible planning claim is the majority of project time, with the excess over half set by how much of the design is new. The scope error lies in the third of the three 70 percent figures §1.3 collects: that one prices validation in the umbrella sense, pre-silicon and post-silicon work together, rather than the pre-silicon verification a plan of this kind budgets. The same source puts post-silicon validation alone at half, which is the figure §1.4 cites, so the umbrella number cannot be spent twice.

**Solution 1.5.** The absent fields are Escape analysis and Cost if escaped. Without the first, the report records what broke and not why the existing suite could not see it, so nothing forces the process change that would prevent the bug's siblings; §1.4 makes that field the reason a bug report is an economic document rather than a defect record. Without the second, the report carries no price for the escape, so a risk register prices this bug at the two engineer-days actually spent rather than at the downstream cost avoided. The omissions match pitfall 6, skipping the escape post-mortem, and pitfall 5, costing a respin as masks-plus-fab. The rest of the write-up is sound, and the found-by line is what makes it so: the 11-cycle counterexample is the evidence the severity line rests on, and it is available because the bug was caught where observability was still total.

## Chapter 2

**Solution 2.1.** Both paths meet at the designer's interpretation, so the split leaves the closed loop of the opening intact. The reconvergence model locates what is actually verified at the point where the two paths meet (§2.1), and the two paths are the RTL and the *checks*, not the RTL and the stimulus: stimulus decides which states get visited, while the expected results decide what counts as correct, so moving stimulus authorship to a second engineer changes the visited set and leaves the verdict on the designer's reading. Writing the assertions and the reference model off the RTL is also the collapse §2.3 names in its own right, a reference reverse-engineered from what the RTL does instead of derived from the specification. The chapter's practice list prescribes the opposite split: the verification plan and the coverage model reviewed by someone else against the specification, the assertions written from the specification text rather than the RTL, and an independent engineer owning the scoreboard's reference model, which breaks the interpretation loop at the checks even when one person writes the stimulus. The deciding artifact is therefore the scoreboard's reference model, with the assertions beside it.

**Solution 2.2.** §2.2 requires the least tolerant observer that the reference clock permits, even though no real receiver would be built that way. The mechanism is that recovery robustness and checking power move in opposite directions: the more tolerant the recovery algorithm, the less checking it performs, because a tolerant monitor adapts to data rates that are out of spec and so locks onto the very deviation it exists to detect, reporting a clean stream. Reusing the receiver IP's algorithm imports exactly that tolerance, and fidelity to a real receiver is not the objective, since verification sets out to check the design as thoroughly as possible even where that means departing from reality (§2.2). On a 10G Ethernet MAC the same mistake takes the form of a receive monitor whose elastic-buffer model absorbs clock drift beyond what the specification permits: it accepts every frame, including the frames a compliant link partner would have rejected.

**Solution 2.3.** The covergroup declares four coverpoints: `cp_stride` with two bins, `cp_bnd` with three, `cp_len` with three, and `cp_ch1` with two. The cross `x_attack` therefore demands 2 × 3 × 3 × 2 = 36 cells. The axis in question is `cp_stride`, whose second bin is `neg`, and §2.2 places it in the model on reachability rather than on intended use: negative stride "is on the list not because anyone plans to use it that way but because the register field is signed, so it can happen". That is the same argument §2.3 has the verifier make to the designer, where the operative question is whether the programming interface can express the case.

**Solution 2.4.** The comment names a 64-bit bus, so the largest legal `awsize` is 3 and one beat carries 2 to the third power = 8 bytes. A burst of 256 beats has `awlen` = 255, since the length field encodes the number of beats minus one, and it moves 256 × 8 = 2048 bytes. The property requires the offset plus that span to stay within the page, so offset + 2048 ≤ 4096 and the largest admissible offset is 4096 − 2048 = 2048: a burst starting there ends exactly on the boundary and satisfies the property, and any larger offset crosses it. The upper edge of the `in_burst` bin is 2048 as well, and the two quantities are not the same quantity: the first is a value of `awaddr[11:0]`, the offset into the page, while the second is a value of `bytes_to_4kb_boundary`, the headroom, which for this offset is 4096 − 2048 = 2048 as well. What they share is their unit, the span of one maximum burst at the largest legal `awsize`, which is why the bin's comment reads "crossing reachable in one burst, or exactly filled by one": the bin boundary is drawn at the reach of a single burst rather than at a round address.

**Solution 2.5.** The summary establishes that the random environment reached every cell of the cross and that no test reported a failure, and it establishes nothing whatever about the burst splitter, because the last line records that no comparison was executed. The burst splitter is the DMA stage that splits each transfer into bursts that the AXI address rules allow. The rule it puts in question is *no coverage without checking* (§2.4): a coverage bin hit while nothing verified the outcome converts ignorance into false confidence and is worse than an empty bin, so once the scoreboard line is read the coverage line becomes a liability rather than evidence. The tests line on its own matches pitfall 4, counting tests instead of checked coverage, since a growing pass rate over an unchecked design measures the testbench's tolerance and nothing else. §2.4 supplies the reading of the whole summary in the sentence it applies to the opening: when everything passes, the first hypothesis is that no check has fired.

## Chapter 3

**Solution 3.1.** The widths sum to 32 + 32 + 32 + 32 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 8 = 192 bits, which is the figure §3.1 reads off the table for one channel. The register set aside is `STATUS`, the read-only byte carrying busy, done and the backend error code, so one channel's programmable count is 192 − 8 = 184 bits and the pair's is 2 × 184 = 368 bits, which is the exponent the chapter's opening uses when it states that stripping the read-only status bits leaves two to the 368th programmable configurations. The sixteen bits that sentence strips are the two `STATUS` bytes, 2 × 8 = 16. The predicate "architecturally visible" attaches to 192 for one channel and 2 × 192 = 384 for the pair, never to 184 or 368, because the visible count includes state that software can read but not write. The two are not interchangeable in an expression of the form two to the Nth because they answer different questions: the visible count bounds the states a checker must be able to distinguish, the programmable count bounds the states stimulus can command, and substituting one for the other silently changes what the exponent claims.

**Solution 3.2.** The class counts multiply to 16 × 16 × 10 × 7 × 4 × 4 × 8 × 4 = 9,175,040, about nine million, which is the figure §3.1 reads off the table; each count in that table carries a tilde, so the product is an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a census. §3.1 calls a suite of a thousand hand-crafted tests exceptional, and one case per test gives 1000 / 9,175,040 = 1.1 × 10<sup>-4</sup> of the product, that is about one case in nine thousand, since 9,175,040 / 1000 = 9,175. The remainder is what §3.1 calls the untested residue, and the section points at Chapter 1's escape data for what that residue does rather than at any further arithmetic. The exercise measures the second of the two reductions §3.1 describes, from about 10<sup>57</sup> raw register states to 10<sup>6</sup> to 10<sup>7</sup> meaningful cases: abstraction removes most of the space, and a directed suite still samples what abstraction leaves at one part in nine thousand, which is why §3.1 puts stimulus automation and measurement after abstraction rather than instead of it.

**Solution 3.3.** The proposal degrades controllability, the first link of the three-link chain of §3.2. The mechanism is the one that section states for this bug: the buggy condition is a one-cycle coincidence between two independently timed events, and at SoC level aligning a timer interrupt with one specific instruction inside a firmware loop is nearly impossible to arrange deliberately and worse to reproduce, whereas at core level the IRQ lines are driven directly and randomization exercises the coincidence across thousands of runs. Observability is not the binding constraint, since the ISS co-simulation oracle compares architectural state instruction by instruction and sees the corrupted flag in the cycle it commits, so the proposal trades away the link the environment cannot recover by other means. The criterion §3.2 sets is that a feature must be verified at a level where the testbench can both provoke it and see it fail, and the proposal matches pitfall 4, verifying at the wrong level, whose symptom is a block corner deferred to SoC tests where it is uncontrollable and unobservable.

**Solution 3.4.** The two readings are that every INCR burst the backend issued stayed inside its 4 KB page, and that the backend issued no INCR burst at all. The projection table of §3.3 states the second in its own row: a backend that hangs forever satisfies the property vacuously, because the implication never triggers and so the property never fails. The question the assertion leaves unanswered is whether a legal transfer ever completes, and §3.3 assigns that question to the scoreboard: for a data-moving design the testbench applies the specified transform to each input transaction, stores the expected output transactions in queues, and compares actual outputs against them as they emerge. The check it performs at end of test is that nothing expected was lost, and that completeness check is what separates a silent backend from a correct one. The table's other rows bound the assertion further, since it owns neither the data the split bursts carry, nor their sum against the programmed transfer, nor the read channel.

**Solution 3.5.** The passing regression establishes that the design and the testbench agree, not that either of them is right, which is the sentence §3.3 closes its oracle discussion on. §3.3 names the failure mode a common-mode error: every oracle encodes someone's reading of the specification, so when the scoreboard's author misreads the same ambiguous sentence the designer misread, model and design agree, the test passes, and the bug is structurally invisible to the environment. The defenses that section prescribes are organizational rather than technical: independent interpretation, with the verifier reading the specification rather than the RTL, review of the oracle itself, and treating every disagreement between model and design as a specification question whose resolution belongs in the specification text. A further oracle of the same kind is not among them because the fault lies not in any oracle's coverage but in the reading they share: an assertion or a second scoreboard written from the same sentence by the same reading agrees with the design for the same reason, so the additional projection multiplies the checks without touching the common cause. §3.3 adds the reason the resolution belongs upstream, since specification issues are among the leading root causes of the functional flaws that force respins.

**Solution 3.6.** The status line is entitled to claim activation and nothing more: the stimulus reached the scenario, and the plan row names no one who decides whether the result was right. The empty field is the oracle field, and the row commits pitfall 3, activation without a checker, whose cure the chapter's pitfall list states as every plan item naming its oracle, the check and not the stimulus. The oracle §3.3 assigns to this bug is the scoreboard's byte-accurate memory model, comparing final memory contents against the transform of the programmed transfer descriptor, the record that programs one DMA transfer. The reason it is the only one is that in the actual bug the emitted bursts were individually legal and the data landed wrong, so nothing fires the assertion that watches the 4 KB boundary, the address line that AXI forbids a burst to cross. The plan row's constrained-random stimulus can visit the cell for an entire regression while the mis-split goes unnoticed, which is the shape of the third escape of §3.2, visible but unrecognized.

## Chapter 4

**Solution 4.1.** The bug row of §4.5 reads 63 filed and 63 dispositioned, and its note splits the dispositions into 58 fixed and regressed, 4 not-a-bug after the specification was clarified, and 1 waived as a documentation erratum: 58 + 4 + 1 = 63, so the three dispositions partition the filed set and none is left undisposed, which is what the closure strand of §4.5 demands when it asks that every open bug have a disposition. The plan-features row closes XB-01 through XB-07, while the plan excerpted in §4.2 lists six rows, XB-01 through XB-06, plus the out-of-scope entry; the table's own note says the seventh was added at plan review. That review is the requirements and plan review of §4.6, whose roster seats design, verification, software and system stakeholders. The hole it caught is the one the scenario of §4.6 records, a use case invisible from inside the block: no scenario in which the debug manager is the only active manager, issuing accesses into a quiesced system, was in the feature list, and the row added for it produced one directed test and one coverage bin that in week 16 caught an arbiter assumption. §4.6 credits the catch to its own principle that completeness needs outsiders, since requirements reviews seat stakeholders from outside the project team.

**Solution 4.2.** §4.2 gives the crossbar's deliverable schedule as environment skeleton 2 weeks, single-path bring-up 1 week, all-paths random with scoreboard 3 weeks, protocol assertion bind-in 1 week and coverage model 2 weeks, so the sum is 2 + 1 + 3 + 1 + 2 = 9 weeks. The saving §4.2 reports runs from 10 to 40 percent of the overall verification schedule, which on that sum gives 10 percent of 9 weeks = 0.9 weeks at the low end and 40 percent of 9 weeks = 3.6 weeks at the high end. The sum is smaller than the schedule the saving applies to because §4.1 lists six phases while this deliverable list reaches only through the build phase and the first bring-up demonstration: the long middle of regression and coverage, closure and sign-off all sit outside it, and §4.3 calls that middle the longest phase of the lifecycle. The qualification §4.2 attaches is explicit, that the figures describe a single organization's reported experience and do not establish a ranking of lifecycle phases by return on investment, so the band indicates where the leverage is rather than supplying a coefficient to multiply a schedule by.

**Solution 4.3.** Stage claims license statements about verification maturity and not about RTL quality, which §4.4 states directly: a block can be at stage 2 with open bugs, because the claim is that the machinery to find and track them is mature rather than that the design is clean, and conflating maturity with cleanliness produces a dashboard of green process indicators over a red design. The inference about bug counts is unlicensed in both directions, and §4.4's own stage-2 line shows why: it claims that the bug-open curve has peaked and that closure is outpacing discovery, which says nothing about the level at which the curve peaked, so the crossbar may well carry more open bugs than the DMA engine. What the crossbar's claim does license is what §4.1's scenario acts on: stage 2 means all planned stimulus and checkers are implemented, the coverage model is implemented and reporting, regression runs at its target cadence with a managed pass rate, and the block is trustworthy enough for others to build on, so the SoC lifecycle gates its own bring-up milestone on the crossbar reaching that stage. The DMA engine's stage 1 licenses only that its plan exists and has passed review, that its environment instantiates the current RTL and drives and checks real transactions, and that a smoke suite passes in regression, so nothing downstream may yet build on its results and the integration order follows from that, not from a comparison of bug counts.

**Solution 4.4.** §4.7 forbids exactly this extrapolation and names the reason: debug is the single largest consumer of verification engineer time, larger than testbench development, test writing or test planning, and debug effort is unpredictable and varies significantly from project to project, which invalidates the most natural estimation method, extrapolating from the last project. What carries across is the machinery rather than the numbers, and §4.7 lists it: schedule in demonstrable deliverables with linear progress so that slippage shows in weeks rather than quarters, buy schedule with planning up front where the leverage is, keep the maturity stages truthful so that a percentage cannot conceal stage 1 until RTL freeze, and protect the regression cadence and the debug loop. The two structural reasons for the spread are both in §4.7: projects at the low end lean on pre-verified IP reuse while projects at the high end carry high proportions of newly developed IP, which is why the crossbar, configured from a mature in-house pattern, and the neural accelerator, new, sit at opposite ends of the reference SoC's effort table despite comparable gate counts. The third variable is team composition, which §4.7 places inside the team and calls a first-order schedule variable that no methodology hides: individual skill spread reaches up to 10 times in productivity across engineers, and best-known methods simply not being in use is estimated to cost around 20 percent of productivity on its own, so an unchanged flow is not an unchanged effort.

**Solution 4.5.** The ticked line the blank field contradicts is the first, coverage closed against the plan, and the ticked line the disabled-test row contradicts is the second, full regression passing over a sustained window. Coverage closed against the plan requires, in §4.4's stage-3 wording, every exclusion written down and reviewed, and §4.5 admits declared exclusions as a feature of closure while forbidding the silent one, so the blank exclusions field is the field §4.5 forbids leaving blank: the crossbar's own summary in §4.5 records its exclusion count with the list attached, and this claim records neither the count nor a reason. Full regression passing over a sustained window fails on the claim's own next line, since §4.5 asks for all tests passing, repeatedly, across seeds rather than a single lucky green run, and the stage-2 checklist excerpt of §4.4 states the same condition as a line reading no test disabled, which this claim contradicts while reporting a pass rate that the disablement is what holds up. The chapter's In practice list requires the reviewer to treat a disabled test as an open bug against the environment, and names temporary disablement to keep the pass rate green as a stage claim without evidence; applied to the last row, that adds two environment bugs to the open-bug count, so 7 filed and 7 dispositioned is no longer the whole set and the third tick, every open bug has a disposition, is not established either. The unticked line is not a defect: §4.4 says a block is at stage N while it works through that stage's checklist and exits into stage N+1 when every line is ticked, so a stage-3 claim with the sign-off review still open is the model being used as intended, and it is the ticks above it, not the empty box, that the reviewer must challenge.

## Chapter 5

**Solution 5.1.** §5.5 quotes a coverage model for a 120-instruction ISA at roughly 3,600 lines of SystemVerilog, a further 9,000 lines for a 300-instruction extension, and a rate of 10 to 30 lines per instruction. The implied rates are 3,600 / 120 = 30 lines per instruction and 9,000 / 300 = 30 lines per instruction, so both totals sit at the top of the band rather than in its middle. At the low end of the same band the two instruction counts would give 120 × 10 = 1,200 lines and 300 × 10 = 3,000 lines, a factor of 3 below the quoted totals; what follows for quoting the totals as estimates is that they are the pessimistic end of a band presented as a single figure, so a plan that treats either as a mean over-provisions by up to that factor, and any schedule built on them should carry the band rather than the point. The conclusion §5.5 reports the source as drawing is that generation, not typing, is the only sane source for that class of model, and the section is careful to attribute both the arithmetic and the conclusion to that program rather than adopting them.

**Solution 5.2.** The blank field is the closure metric, and the rule that governs it is §5.2's one row, one metric: the field states the single measurable exit condition that closes the row, and a row needing two kinds of evidence is two rows, which is why §5.2 splits DMA-F06 into F06a and F06b and DMA-N01 into N01a and N01b, giving two features, four rows and four metrics. The failing field is the pass/fail criterion, and §5.2 rules out exactly this shape of sentence: it says that transfer completes is not a criterion, and that the field must state how the response will be judged correct and above all which errors to look for, so that an engineer who is not the block's expert can build a reliable check. Restated in the chapter's own pattern, the criterion reads: every destination byte equals its source byte, in order, exactly once, for every descriptor in the chain, and the done interrupt follows the last accepted write response of the last descriptor. The row also carries a feature name where §5.2 requires attributes, the dimensions and the values that matter, so the chain lengths and the sensitive values that select the interesting paths are absent, and the criterion above cannot be measured until they are named. The cross-reference the row lacks is the one §5.3 builds from the permanent identifier, tying the specification clause the feature came from to the testcases and properties that discharge it and the coverage points that measure it; §5.3 states the consequence flatly, that a feature with no cross-reference is not being verified.

**Solution 5.3.** The attribute list of DMA-F06a gives stride sign in three values, the 2D row's relation to the next boundary in four, burst length in three and the other DMA channel in two, so `x_worst` has 3 × 4 × 3 × 2 = 72 cells. A 2D row is the contiguous run of bytes that makes up one line of a two-dimensional transfer, and the stride is the signed address step from the start of one row to the start of the next. §5.2 states that full coverage means 100 percent of the cells the model can legitimately reach, fewer than 72, and excludes the six maximum-length straddle cells, so 72 - 6 = 66 cells count toward the plan row's closure. The premise the exclusion rests on is the generator's constraint solver budget, which caps a 2D row at 256 beats: a straddling 2D row therefore splits into pieces none of which can itself be a maximum-length burst. That premise belongs to a decision the team took and not to the model, since §5.2 says lifting the budget brings the cell back, and it is recorded on the plan row itself as a waiver with its justification rather than in a footnote. The same passage fixes the bug's footprint at two of the 72 cells and not three, `neg` crossed with `straddle` and the other channel active, in the one-beat and the 255-beat cells, because the 256-beat cell is one of the excluded ones. The reasons the Chapter 2 model counts fewer cells for the same feature are two refinements rather than a disagreement: its stride axis has one bin fewer, because a zero stride, with every 2D row starting at the same address, is its own splitting path rather than a degenerate case of the other two, and its boundary axis bins headroom in absolute bytes against fixed thresholds while this model bins it relative to what one burst of this 2D row would move, a frontier that moves with the burst length and that a fixed threshold cannot identify.

**Solution 5.4.** The row stays where it is, on the must-not-happen list of §5.1. That list carries the relevant and probable errors the environment must be able to detect, and a done interrupt raised before the last write response is accepted is an error of exactly that kind, one that software reading a status register cannot see; the designer's ground is a statement about one piece of software rather than about the design. §5.1 requires of every out-of-scope entry that it be a reviewed decision rather than an accident or an unexamined hope, written down with its review recorded, which is the form the DMA-X01 entry takes when it names a documented software constraint reviewed with design in week 3. The second question the verification lead asks in §5.4 is whether the RTL checks the condition or assumes it, and in the chapter's scenario the answer is that it assumes it; the same answer here means the row's risk rank rises rather than falls, because an unchecked assumption reachable by software the block's team does not control is where escapes breed, and §5.4 adds the companion move of a new must-not-happen row on the frontend for descriptors the design cannot honor. The lead's first question applies as well: the register interface is documented and other teams write their own descriptors, so the driver is not the only software that will ever program the block, and the interrupt can be enabled by code the driver's authors do not own.

**Solution 5.5.** §5.5 puts a per-feature decision in place of a uniform template, taken on two axes, the consequence of an escape and the likelihood of a bug, on top of the priority tiers that pre-draw the schedule cut line: must-have features define first-time success and get thorough verification across all configurations, should-have features get their basic operation verified with depth added only as time allows, and nice-to-have features are verified as time allows, which §5.5 notes today's schedules almost guarantee means never. Walked across DMA-F06 the five drivers read: novelty high, since the 2D negative-stride path is new rather than silicon-proven reuse; structural complexity high, since the splitter's resumption arithmetic sits in state shared between two channels; specification quality high, since the document is silent on this case; controllability and observability low, since descriptors are programmed directly and every issued burst is visible on the backend interface; and cost of escape at the worst available, silent data corruption that no status flag reports. That ranking buys the depth §5.5 spends when consequence and likelihood are both high, in every currency at once: two rows, a full cross in constrained-random simulation for the transport and a formal safety property for the invariant, with tighter closure criteria and a review of the checker itself. DMA-F01 inverts every answer, being reused register logic generated with its own tests from one description through a register abstraction layer, with a fully specified access policy, complete observability and a consequence software can read back, so its row deliberately buys almost nothing: generated register tests and a review of the generator configuration. The favorable driver does not reduce DMA-F06's depth because §5.5 states its role precisely, that good controllability and observability make depth affordable rather than unnecessary; depth is set by the product of consequence and likelihood, and both of those stay high whatever the observability.

## Chapter 6

**Solution 6.1.** §6.2 reports a five-state machine with 14 possible transitions on which a single sequence reaches 100 percent state coverage while covering only 36 percent of the transitions, so the sequence traversed 14 × 36 / 100 = 5.04 transitions, that is 5 of them, since a traversed-arc count is an integer. The count checks back: 5 / 14 = 0.357, that is 35.7 percent, which rounds to the 36 the section reports, while the next integer up gives 6 / 14 = 0.429, that is 42.9 percent, which does not. A single sequence that visits every one of the five states must traverse at least 5 - 1 = 4 arcs, so this sequence traversed one arc more than the minimum and either closed a loop or revisited a state once. The two things §6.2 says the metric cannot decide on its own follow from where the graph comes from: because the transitions are extracted from the implementation, the tool cannot identify unintended transitions or intended transitions that are missing, so the graph needs review against the specification, and it cannot decide which of the transitions it reports are unreachable under the protocols feeding the machine, which the section assigns to a property checker.

**Solution 6.2.** §6.6 declares the tensor width as `logic [8:0]`, a nine-bit field, and §6.3 gives `auto_bin_max` a default of 64, so automatic binning would slice 2^9 = 512 values into 64 bins holding 512 / 64 = 8 values each. The bin name §6.3 quotes for the lane boundary, `auto[16:23]`, fixes the alignment at multiples of eight, so the bin containing the architectural maximum begins at 256, since 256 / 8 = 32 whole bins precede it, and runs from 256 up to 256 + 8 - 1 = 263. What else falls inside that bin is the range 257 to 263, and the line that decides how the model must treat those values is `illegal_bins out_of_range` on `cp_width`, which declares the legal range to be 1 through 256 and everything outside it, the zero below as well as the values above, a value the register checker must reject, so one reaching the datapath is a bug rather than a hole; §6.6 adds that illegal values are excluded from coverage, which is why `cp_width` still offers six bins. Automatic binning would therefore merge the architectural maximum with values that must never occur, in the same way §6.3 shows it merging widths 16 and 17, exactly the two the datapath treats differently, and both merges are the chapter's second pitfall, automatic bins on meaningful fields.

**Solution 6.3.** The result establishes that the modeled space was sampled and nothing about whether the design is correct, and §6.4 states the property that forces the distinction: verification aims to remove bugs, while coverage closure is only a well-defined, measurable derivative of that goal, and a derivative can be optimized directly, so a campaign whose stimulus is tuned only for bin hits can reach 100 percent with inactive checkers and no evidence that misbehavior would have been detected. The same section supplies the division of labor that the tool leaves untouched: coverage measures sampling while checkers establish correctness, and coverage methodology assumes that an orthogonal mechanism detects device misbehavior, so without output comparisons a full report merely counts jobs. The stopping criteria of §6.8 the overnight result does not by itself satisfy are three, and none of them is a percentage: a full reviewed model, a current exclusion file, and a stable period without model refinements, since both stimulus and model evolve during closure and §6.8 treats the discovery that new attributes are needed as part of that refinement. The reason §6.8 gives for tightening the checking review is that automation makes machine-optimized 100 percent arrive faster and with even less human examination of the scenarios, which is what turns §6.4's requirement into a gate: the coverage-model review must include the checkers observing the same events and identify, for every coverpoint, who detects misbehavior in that scenario.

## Chapter 8

**Solution 8.1.** §8.6 gives the crossbar five managers, four subordinates and ID width 4, so a manager-side ID field takes 2⁴ = 16 values, and the Step 4 key (manager port, direction, manager-side ID) addresses 5 × 2 × 16 = 160 queues. The `stream_key` function of the same step confirms the layout: the ID occupies the low four bits, the direction bit 4 and the manager the three bits above it, so the encoding is injective and the 160 live keys sit inside 8 × 2 × 16 = 256 addressable slots. The rejected two-coordinate key (subordinate, ID) addresses 4 × 16 = 64. Step 4 holds that the shorter key fails all three concurrency sources, and each failure has its own reason: the key carries no manager coordinate, so the five managers issuing at once collapse into whichever subordinate they reached; it carries no direction coordinate, so `ARID` and `AWID`, which name unrelated streams, merge into one space; and its identifier coordinate is therefore not per port and per direction, so the ordering the protocol guarantees, per-ID ordering within one port and one direction, is not the ordering one of its queues enforces. The signature Step 4 attributes to two managers holding the same identifier at once is a mismatch that disappears on a seed change rather than a reproducible bug: manager 1 and manager 2 may both have ID 3 outstanding to subordinate 0, and one queue then matches one manager's expectation against the other's response, under some arbitrations only. The merged identifier spaces carry a second and distinct signature, a read expectation popped by a write response.

**Solution 8.2.** Each of the five manager ports issues a 4-bit ID, so 2⁴ = 16 identities per port and 5 × 16 = 80 distinct stream identities per direction. Step 4 derives the subordinate-side field from the same two figures: five managers need three manager-identifying bits, and 4 + 3 = 7, so the widened field encodes 2⁷ = 128 values, of which the remap uses 80. The map is therefore injective, and the widening exists for that reason, to avoid falsely serializing different managers' same-ID transactions, but which of the 128 encodings a given manager and manager-side ID land on is the design's ID-remap policy. Step 4 states the consequence: keying at the subordinate side is sound only with the widened ID, which obliges the predictor to reproduce that remap policy exactly, so the checker acquires a second implementation of a design decision and a disagreement about it becomes indistinguishable from a routing bug. Manager-side keying is preferred because the key stays computable from what the observer sees: the monitor reads the port it is attached to, the direction and the ID the manager issued, and no part of the key depends on modeling the design.

**Solution 8.3.** The comparison establishes that the environment's own responders replied as the environment told them to, and §8.6's Step 3 says so directly: comparing against a subordinate-side response monitor checks the environment's own work. What it cannot establish is the crossbar's return path, because read data flows from subordinate through crossbar to manager, so a comparison taken before the crossbar cannot show whether subordinate 0's read data reached manager 2 rather than manager 3 in ID order. This is why Step 3 places the return-path check on the manager side and Step 4 keys it there. Of Step 0's verdicts, the two the scoreboard owns lose their mechanism: data integrity per ordered stream, and completeness, meaning nothing lost and nothing invented. Routing correctness survives, since Step 3's forward-path check is the comparison that legitimately uses the subordinate-side request monitor, and protocol legality survives with Step 2's per-interface checkers. The proposal therefore does not weaken the environment evenly: it removes exactly the verdict that only a manager-side observation can produce, and leaves an environment that still reports green.

**Solution 8.4.** `report()` returns the conjunction of its three zero tests, so with the leftover field nonzero it returns 0 and also raises an error naming the count, even though the orphan and mismatch counts are 0 and every one of the 256 responses that arrived matched its expectation. The behavior the nonzero field records is data the design did not deliver: §8.6's fourth detail states that a response that never arrives produces no mismatch, only a queue that never empties, so `leftover()` is what makes an otherwise silent failure visible, and §8.3 gives the general reading, that data remaining at simulation end was lost in the design. What the count leaves undetermined is whether that loss is an error: §8.3 says so in the same sentence, and gives the check that resolves it for an ordered stream, confirming assumed drops against the design's own drop-count statistics. The count also says nothing about where in the run the loss happened, which is what the capture index and prediction time of Step 6 are for. An environment without the field matches pitfall 4, no end-of-test completeness check, whose symptom is exactly this shape: every produced transaction matches, so the test passes while missing transactions go unnoticed.

**Solution 8.5.** The substitution costs the detection of undriven data. §8.6's fourth detail gives the rule: logical inequality on operands containing x or z yields `1'bx`, and `if` reads that value as false, so with `!=` a beat queue containing a single undriven beat takes the else branch and scores as matched. The failure made invisible is therefore a beat the design never drove, and it is made invisible in the direction that matters: the check reports a match rather than an error, so nothing in the log distinguishes it from a clean run. The case comparison `!==` compares x and z as values and reports the difference, which is why the scoreboard uses it. The other guard of the same step is `$isunknown`, applied to the ID at both entry points, and it defends against the same class of value in the key rather than in the payload: `int'(id)` is a two-state cast, so without the guard an unknown ID collapses to 0 and the transaction is filed under a real key, corrupting the queues of that stream. Both guards exist because a two-state view of a four-state value silently substitutes a legal answer for an unknown one.

**Solution 8.6.** The substitution costs the environment its only golden oracle. §8.7 states the scope precisely: the descriptor model is golden because data-mover transfer semantics are the specification, and because it implements them at byte-movement abstraction rather than burst abstraction, so it predicts only where bytes end up and excludes splitting altogether. The burst-splitter model cannot inherit that status because it predicts those split decisions, which makes it a transfer function, and §8.4 states what a transfer function is: it does not exist a priori, is not golden by definition, and will contain as many errors as the design itself, so a disagreement first requires deciding which of the two implementations misread the specification. §8.7 draws the conclusion for this block, that the two models for one IP differ in the abstraction level each commits to, and that omitting the splitting details is what keeps the descriptor model maintainable. The over-specification §8.7 warns against is checking exact split sequences: doing so turns every legitimate change to the burst splitter's arbitration into a regression failure, so the checks it prescribes instead are that bursts stay within one 4 KB region each, cover the descriptor exactly once in aggregate and match byte counts, with coverage recording which split shapes appeared. Pitfall 5 names the same defect in general form, the model that predicts arbitration order, latency or burst decomposition and breaks the regression on legal design changes.

## Chapter 9

**Solution 9.1.** §9.3 writes the constraint as `s -> d == 0` over a 1-bit `s` and a 32-bit `d`. When `s` is true the implication forces one solution, `d == 0`; when `s` is false `d` is unconstrained and contributes 2³² solutions, so the legal set has 1 + 2³² members, and `s` is true in exactly one of them. The value `d == 0` occurs in two of those members, once with `s` true and once with `s` false, so under uniformity over legal combinations its probability is 2 / (1 + 2³²), which §9.3 calls effectively never. Adding `solve s before d` leaves the legal set identical and only changes the order in which the solver picks: `s` is chosen first over its two values, so `s` is true with probability 1 / 2 and then `d == 0` is forced, and `s` false still yields `d == 0` with probability 1 / 2³², giving 1 / 2 + (1 / 2) × (1 / 2³²) = 1 / 2 + 1 / 2³³. That second term is the whole content of the word `slightly`: the figure exceeds one half by the chance that a freely chosen `d` happens to land on zero, which is why §9.3 writes slightly over 50 percent rather than exactly half. Because the legal set is unchanged, §9.3 notes that ordering, like `dist`, cannot cause the solver to fail.

**Solution 9.2.** The DMA's data path is 64 bits, so 64 / 8 = 8 bytes per beat. The row starts 4096 - 4088 = 8 bytes below the frontier, so the first piece is 8 / 8 = 1 beat; the remainder is 4104 - 8 = 4096 bytes, that is 4096 / 8 = 512 beats, which exceeds the 256-beat maximum burst and therefore splits again into 256 + 256. One piece of a straddling row is thus a maximum-length burst, so the combination of the `straddle` bin with the `max` bin becomes reachable, which is exactly the column §9.3 had shown to be empty. The premise the example lifts is `c_solver_budget`, which caps a row at 2048 bytes, that is 2048 / 8 = 256 beats, one maximum burst and half a page; §9.3 states that a capped row reaches past at most one frontier and splits into exactly two pieces whose beat counts sum to at most 256, each at least one, so neither piece can be 256. The cap is an engineering choice about solve time rather than a property of the design, so §9.3 requires two things on vplan row DMA-F06a: that the six exclusions be recorded as depending on policy and revisited when the budget moves, and that the argument be marked destination-side, since `c_no_page_cross`, `c_addressable` and `dst_row[]` all speak about writes. Chapter 5's rule that 100 percent means 100 percent of the cells the model can legitimately reach is what makes the bookkeeping mandatory rather than optional, and §9.3 names the failure it prevents, a sign-off that quotes 72.

**Solution 9.3.** The weights had no effect because they were written over a raw field rather than over the quantity the coverage model measures. §9.3 shapes the expression the coverpoint samples: `c_frontier` distributes where row 0 ends modulo 4 KB, which is the relation `cp_boundary_dist` classifies, rather than the destination address by itself. The reason the choice matters is that the coverpoint bins a relation between the address and the row length, so a distribution over the address alone leaves the length free to move the relation anywhere inside a bin, and the report is unmoved for the same reason pitfall 5 gives, weights added, report unmoved, with its cure to write the distribution over the same expression the coverpoint samples. Two limits remain even when the constraint is written correctly. First, it targets one bin of four and not all four: §9.3 states that the third range maps onto `straddle` while the first two land somewhere among the remaining bins without choosing which, because those bins are classified against a burst's worth of headroom rather than against the frontier. Second, the expression names row 0 only, while `cp_boundary_dist` samples every row, so the shaping acts on the first of up to sixty-four rows; §9.3 adds that coverage reports are what establish whether two distributions over overlapping expressions achieved their intended effect, since the standard leaves the interaction between `dist` and solve order undefined.

**Solution 9.4.** The insertion broke random stability. §9.4 gives the mechanism that makes a seed reusable: the generator is localized to threads and objects, each class instance receives its own generator seeded from the thread that creates it and each new dynamic thread from its parent, so the values one object or thread produces are independent of the others. Stability then holds only as long as object creation, thread creation and random-number generation happen in the same order as before, and a draw inserted at the head of a sequence shifts every later draw in that thread, which is why §9.4 says one such call can change an entire regression. The call had to be appended after the existing ones, at the end of the code block, which §9.4 states preserves the earlier sequence and keeps previously created work reproducible. The same rule governs the two other kinds of addition, new objects and new threads, for the same reason: both consume a position in the creation order that later constructions inherit. Hierarchical seeding is what fixes the scope: because each new thread is seeded from its parent, manually seeding one root thread pins a whole subsystem, which is the unit a reproduction command can therefore fix.

**Solution 9.5.** The shape of the report already answers check 1, checking the generator. §9.3 states the discriminator: a whole axis value absent from every combination it appears in, rather than scattered missing cells, indicates a disabled stimulus dimension, and the report shows exactly that, one value of one axis empty while every value of the other three axes is hit. The report also carries the second half of check 1, that a functional-coverage gap can indicate a fault in the generator or the environment rather than in the design, and the header line supplies the corroboration by recording no test failures: nothing is complaining, which is the signature of stimulus that never arrives rather than of behavior that misbehaves. For the axis at fault, check 2 asks which generator variable changes each axis, and §9.3 records what it finds for `cp_boundary_dist`: only a pinning constraint exists, which is `c_no_page_cross`, so no variable of the generator is free to move that axis at all. An owner and a due date is the wrong response because it treats the empty block as a difficult corner, which is the misreading §9.3 records as the outcome of this report, the block receiving an owner and a due date and remaining unresolved. The right response is check 3, requesting the relation that defines the missing cell so that a randomization failure names the conflict, and then removing the constraint, since §9.3 shows the request satisfiable in the base descriptor class and unsatisfiable in the over-constrained one. Pitfall 6 names the variant to avoid while waiting, raising the seed count against a hole that constraints have closed.

**Solution 9.6.** The report describes permitted behavior, not a defect. §9.6 states that `parallel` guarantees a synchronized start and the absence of any scheduling dependency across branches, and that two executions with no dependency between them may or may not overlap in time, from which §9.6 draws the conclusion directly: a contention scenario may produce no contention. The proposal is worse than ineffective, because §9.6 states that capacity constrains generation rather than imposing a queueing delay: the reference SoC has two channels, the pool is sized accordingly, and reducing it to one makes the activity illegal, so a scenario that would over-subscribe the pool is not generated at all rather than being generated and serialized. The construct that permits an entirely sequential order is `schedule`, which §9.6 says allows any legal order, including branches run one after another, so it widens rather than narrows the set of orders the tool may pick. Pool depth therefore acts on generation in both directions: it bounds how many channel-locking actions may hold a channel at one time, each with a distinct instance, and a request beyond that bound removes the scenario from the space instead of delaying it. Getting the overlap the team wants belongs elsewhere, in the launching arrangement rather than in the pool, which is the same distinction §9.5 draws when it says constraints specify relations but do not schedule execution.

## Chapter 10

**Solution 10.1.** §10.2 makes an active agent a group of three components, a sequencer, a driver and a monitor, and the crossbar environment holds 5 + 4 = 9 of them, so at block level it builds 3 × 9 = 27 leaf components, nine of each kind. Passive mode suppresses the construction of the sequencer and the driver and leaves the monitor, so the SoC environment of §10.8 builds 9 and 27 − 9 = 18 components cease to exist. The instance override of §10.3 names one full instance path, so it replaces 1 driver and the remaining 9 − 1 = 8 agents keep the standard one; a type override of the same pair of types matches every construction of that type and would therefore replace all 9, which is the difference §10.3 draws between targeting an instance and applying a policy. After the switch to passive neither form replaces anything, 0 in both cases, and the mechanism that fixes this is the moment of resolution: §10.3 states that the factory resolves an override when the child is constructed, so an override of a component nobody constructs is never consulted. The four responding agents are inside the nine and are configured active at block level, because by Chapter 8's criterion a component that answers the crossbar's requests under testbench control is a driver rather than an observer.

**Solution 10.2.** The sequencer is using the default mode, plain FIFO, and the property that produces the delay is that plain FIFO grants in arrival order and reads no priority at all, so the three mainline sequences queued first are served first however the interrupt sequence is ranked. §10.5 lists six modes and identifies the two strict ones, strict FIFO and strict random, as the only modes that grant the highest-priority request first; of the other four, the weighted mode grants randomly by weight, the plain random mode grants randomly, plain FIFO grants by arrival, and the user-defined hook grants by whatever the project writes. This is pitfall 3 of the chapter, and its cure is to select a strict mode rather than to raise the priority further. Of the two further controls, prioritization is the less disruptive and `grab` with `ungrab` the more so: prioritization leaves every other sequence eligible for the grants the interrupt sequence does not take, whereas a grab gives one sequence exclusive use of the sequencer and stalls the rest for as long as it is held, which is why §10.5 presents the grab as the canonical interrupt-service pattern and prioritization as the gentler alternative. A sequence started with a parent sequence as its second argument takes that parent's priority rather than the root default of 100, so a child of the virtual sequence of §10.5 runs at the virtual sequence's priority and the parent's `pre_do`, `mid_do` and `post_do` hooks fire around it.

**Solution 10.3.** `update` compares each register's desired value against its mirrored value and drives bus cycles only where the two differ (§10.6). If `set` has given every register of the block a value unlike its mirror, the comparison holds throughout and the cost is one write per register: 40 registers differing = 40 front-door write cycles, exactly the cost of writing the block register by register. If `set` has left every desired value equal to its mirror, the comparison holds nowhere: 0 registers differing = 0 write cycles, and `update` returns without touching the bus. The count is therefore fixed by the number of registers whose desired and mirrored values disagree and not by the size of the block, which is what makes the model cheaper than forty writes in the ordinary case where a test changes a handful of fields. On a volatile field the write that can be wrongly suppressed is the one the test asked for: §10.6 states that a volatile field can change without the model seeing it, so the mirror may already be stale, and a desired value that happens to equal a stale mirror compares equal and produces no cycle while the design holds something else. The property at work is the one §10.6 attaches to the mirror itself, that it records only what the model believes from the accesses it has seen, and nothing entitles it to be right about a field the design maintains on its own.

**Solution 10.4.** The model needs explicit prediction, which adds a predictor per bus interface, fed by that interface's monitor and looking up the register behind every observed address; §10.8 names explicit prediction as the fix for the register model at system level and §10.6 gives the reason, that an implicitly predicted mirror is updated only after operations the model issued itself, so firmware writes arriving through the crossbar and the APB bridge never reach it and the mirror is wrong within microseconds. The block-level check carries two defects, and only one of them is repaired by changing the prediction mode. The first belongs to §10.6 and was already present at block level: the overrun field is volatile, so comparing it against the mirror without a fresh read is not a check at any level of hierarchy, since the model has no standing to believe the value it last saw. The second belongs to §10.8: the check inherited the assumption that every access to the register originated in the environment, firmware now originates them, and the source of truth therefore moves from what the environment issued to what the monitor observed. §10.6 refuses the mirror the scoreboard role on a separate ground, that a mirror can predict a register the design does not update but cannot judge whether an updated value is right, so model and design may agree on a value both got wrong. §10.5 adds that a scoreboard keyed on the subordinate side loses its key when two managers write under one identifier: the crossbar widens identifiers on the way out, so the two writes arrive at the subordinate under different ones and the key no longer groups what it was meant to group; the interleaving check is unaffected, because it reasons about the order of beats on the write-data channel and never inspects an identifier.

**Solution 10.5.** The policy is `"W1C"`, write-one-to-clear: a written one clears the matching bit, and a read leaves the field alone (§10.6). The two statements the descriptor makes are about different things. `volatile` set to one speaks about the mirror: the field may change where the model cannot see it, so the mirrored value carries no entitlement and a comparison against it without a fresh read establishes nothing. The access policy speaks about prediction: the model computes the post-write value from the policy, so if the real design also clears the flag when software reads it, the register is modeled wrongly, the mirror diverges at every read, and the resulting mismatches are charged to the model rather than to the design. The ignored argument is `is_rand`, passed as 0 in the descriptor: §10.6 records that for any predefined policy outside the six the class reference lists, and `"W1C"` is outside that list, the library switches the field's randomization off whatever the argument holds, so the `rand` qualifier on the declaration records intent and editing the argument alone would not restore randomization. The test §10.6 builds from the two access paths reads the field through the front door, `peek`s it through the back door and compares the two: in the canonical UART bug the front-door read followed by the clearing write leaves the model believing the flag is down while the back-door `peek` shows it still raised, and that disagreement is the firmware lockup made visible in two lines.

## Chapter 11

**Solution 11.1.** In a block-level environment for the DMA engine the clause is an `assume`: the responses arrive at the DMA from outside, so the rule constrains the environment and the directive records where the obligation sits rather than changing what gets checked. In a block-level environment for the crossbar the same text is an `assert`: the crossbar issues those responses, so the clause is an obligation on the design being verified. §11.3 attaches this reason to the practice of writing a module's input rules and output guarantees explicitly, that an integration mistake then shows up as an assumption violation on the first system-level run, at the boundary where the two parties disagree, instead of as a symptom somewhere downstream. To a proof engine the two directives are not symmetric: an assertion is either proved or answered with a counterexample, and it is proved over the traces that survive the assumptions, while an assumption is never itself checked and instead removes traces from consideration. An assumption stronger than the real environment therefore deletes legal traces, and the assertions are established only over what is left, so the proof is a statement about a design narrower than the one that will be built; §11.3 makes that asymmetry the reason for reviewing the directive together with the property, and Chapter 14 turns it into assumption hygiene.

**Solution 11.2.** The length field on the maximum address carries one less than the beat count, so its value is 256 − 1 = 255, the constant `c_max_burst` matches. The checker counts only beats before `WLAST`, so at the tick that last beat is accepted `w_beats` holds 256 − 1 = 255 as well, and `w_beats != aw_len_q[0]` is false with 255 on both sides, meaning no error is flagged. A burst of one beat has a length field of 1 − 1 = 0, and its single beat is already the last, so `w_beats` was never incremented and still holds 0; the comparison is false again with 0 on both sides. **Solution 16.1.** The proof entitles the report to close the plan row it discharges and to leave the cross bins exactly as they stand. §16.3 sorts the pairing into its third disposition, the column of things that must never be summed: a functional coverage bin against a proof, because a hit count and a proof answer different questions, the bin counting occurrences of a situation in the executed runs and the proof holding that no legal trace violates a rule, which is a statement about the testbench and a statement about the design respectively. ## Chapter 17

**Solution 17.1.** One legal burst is the case *N* = 64, so the fraction of peak is 64 / (64 + 200) = 64 / 264 = 0.242, about 24%. The batching table's middle column gives 7.4% for a short burst of 16 cycles and 56% for the 256 beats the protocol encodes, so 24% falls between the second and third rows, which is what §17.4 states of a single call on the AXI write data channel; the reason the third row is out of reach is arithmetic rather than protocol, since a beat is 64 bytes and 64 × 64 = 4,096 bytes, exactly the one 4 KB region the burst splitter permits. The speedup is *S*(*N*) = *N* (1 + *L*) / (*N* + *L*), which tends to 1 + *L* = 201 as *N* grows, and at *N* = *L* it is 200 × 201 / (200 + 200) = 100.5 = 201 / 2, half the ceiling, as §17.4 states. The legal burst therefore leaves the half-ceiling point out of reach as well as the ceiling: *S*(64) = 64 × 201 / 264 = 48.7, well under 100.5, because 64 is well under *L* = 200. That is why §17.4 says the gap to the third row is not something a longer burst can close: the burst is capped by legality, the remedy is a coarser transaction such as a descriptor, and the general rule is that granularity should be comparable to round-trip cost.

**Solution 17.2.** The evidence closes the protocol-state half of the criterion and leaves the timing at the pins open, which is the split §17.3 states for a row closed by an instrument-driven compliance suite. The platform element that forces it is the speed adapter: the design executes at megahertz while the real link runs at its specified rate, so the adapter buffers and throttles traffic and re-times it, and the design receives real content at adapter-generated timing. Timing observed at the pins is therefore the adapter's, not the design's, and those rows stay open until silicon, which is why §17.3 says that a row recording a compliance pass without the split is claiming evidence it does not have, and why the sixth pitfall of the chapter's list names exactly this record. The column left empty is the image the row runs on, which §17.5 adds to Chapter 5's method field for every row that runs on hardware. Without it the row cannot be batched, and batching by image is the whole scheduling mechanism: the image is the scheduling unit, a low-priority row that fits an image already being built is nearly free, a high-priority row needing its own instrumentation costs a build, and §17.5 asks for images to be planned before rows are assigned because reversing the order leaves top-priority rows spread across unscheduled builds.

**Solution 17.3.** The two sections divide the cases. §17.2's amortization holds for an image reused across many hours or days of execution, and across regressions and campaigns: the build is paid once and divided by everything that runs on it, so a build supporting one run bears the full cost while a build supporting two hundred runs is amortized over the group. §17.5 states the other case in the placement model: the build *B* is paid per design revision, as is every nightly suite. A smoke tier exists to test a new revision, so every invocation of the tier is a new revision and a new build, and *B* is paid per invocation and never amortized; against a tier that §17.2 puts at under nine minutes end to end, most of it build, a build measured in hours to days makes the proposal slower by orders of magnitude, and the third pitfall of the chapter's list names it as buying capacity for a farm problem. The property that would reverse the decision is run length rather than run count: a workload whose single run exceeds simulation's reach, since a license pool shortens suites and never shortens a run. §17.5's admission criteria place such a workload in the first claim, workloads with no simulation answer, and a workload valued instead for iteration count under one image in the second, where the build cost is amortized across the sweep. The smoke tier has neither property, and §17.5's diagnostic cases add the reason to look elsewhere first: if profiling shows the design is a minority of runtime, the problem and the fix are software.

**Solution 17.4.** The machine figure implies 2.4 hours = 2.4 × 3,600 = 8,640 seconds of execution for 4,800 runs, that is 8,640 / 4,800 = 1.8 seconds per run, which agrees with the under two seconds §17.5 quotes at the optimistic end of the speed band. The farm figure follows from the same two counts: 4,800 runs × 30 minutes = 144,000 minutes = 2,400 CPU-hours, and 2,400 / 240 = 10 hours of wall clock on 240 licenses, which is the *N·s*/*W*<sub>sim</sub> term of the placement model. The term that decides against the move is *B*, the build, which §17.5 measures in hours to a day and which is paid per design revision, so a nightly suite pays it nightly: 2.4 hours of machine time plus a build of that size, incurred every night, against 10 hours on licenses the project already holds, yields no gain. Parallel width cannot offset it because *W*<sub>hw</sub> is capacity divided by design size rather than a quantity that can be purchased, and §17.5 puts it at 1 for this image, so the 4,800 runs serialize on the one machine while the farm's width is what produced the 10-hour figure in the first place. At the pessimistic end of the band, §17.5 adds, the emulator loses on run time alone, before any build is counted.

**Solution 17.5.** The oracle belongs to the third of §17.4's three classes: an oracle that needs to see every beat costs one host interaction per beat and does not scale at all, so it has to be redesigned rather than ported. That is why the throughput belongs to the oracle: one interaction per beat is the *N* = 1 case of §17.4's arithmetic, which gives *f*/(1 + *L*) and throws away between 95% and 99.95% of peak, so the rate the run advances at is fixed by the granularity of the checking and the round-trip cost, and the platform's clock rate does not enter. The record's second symptom points the same way, since no assertions were compiled into the image, and §17.2 makes those the one form of checking that crosses the boundary intact, costing logic and no interactions and needing no conversation with the host. §17.4 prescribes moving the per-cycle work onto the platform: a synthesizable digest accumulator hashes the ciphertext stream and the authentication tags, the host reads and compares one digest per checkpoint rather than one value per beat, and the per-beat comparison stays a short-vector check in the simulation tier. The cost §17.4 attaches is detection distance: a digest mismatch says the stream diverged somewhere in the last checkpoint interval and not where, so the checkpoint interval is what bounds the debug bisection, and the fifth pitfall of the chapter's list states the alternative, a per-beat scoreboard pinning throughput at transaction rate while the soak never finishes.

**Solution 17.6.** §17.3 licenses the use model for the campaign, because it licenses in-circuit emulation exactly when the environment cannot be modeled, and a third-party host the team does not own is one of the environments it names as justifying the acceptance of irreproducibility, alongside a compliance instrument and a driver stack the team does not own. The two losses that the gating requirement makes decisive are repeatability, since a real link partner's failure has no seed and no replay, and unattended operation, since someone must plug in the device; Chapter 12's stability check repeats a test before it may gate, and §17.3 states that Chapter 12's apparatus assumes a run is a function of recorded inputs and that a failure which cannot be reproduced cannot be triaged. ## Chapter 21

**Solution 21.1.** The block is the 12-bit SAR ADC of the chapter's opening, so full scale is 4096 codes and 1% of it is 4096 / 100 = 40.96, about 41 LSB, which is the figure the model register notes. In volts, one LSB against the 1.8 V reference is 1.8 / 4096 ≈ 439 µV, the value the opening derives, so the band is 41 × 439 = 17,999 µV, about 18 mV. The package states the same band directly, 0.01 × 1.8 = 0.018 V, that is 18 mV, so the register's code form and the package's volt form are one band written twice, and the agreement is what lets a checker quote the constant while the plan row quotes the criterion. The form in use is the absolute one, since the percentage is taken of the fixed full-scale reference and therefore yields a constant band across the whole input range, which §21.5 gives as the reason the correlation check uses the absolute token. The other form, a percentage of the measured value, is the relative tolerance §21.1 says fails near a legitimate zero: taken of a measurement near the bottom of the range it shrinks with the signal and collapses toward zero, so the band would be far narrower there than the 439 µV of a single code.

**Solution 21.2.** The blank fields are the date and owner and the consequence, and each blocks a distinct claim. Without the date and owner the entry cannot support the claim that the correlation is current, because §21.4 makes the date what turns "the model is correlated" into a claim that can visibly go stale, and it cannot name who must re-run correlation when a trigger fires, a division §21.5 states as the analog engineer writing and correlating the model while the verification team owns its register row and its expiry. Without the consequence field the entry cannot support any statement about what else would be wrong if the model were wrong: §21.4 makes that field the answer to the only question a reviewer needs to ask about a model, and the filled form for the ADC names every ADC-path result in the chip-level regression and every plan row that closes on an ADC measurement. The conditions line covers nominal supply and one temperature point, which disqualifies the model from any run outside that nominal box, and the class §21.4 names for exactly this case is a power-management campaign: a model correlated at nominal supply says nothing about behavior at the corners of that supply, so an entire campaign can run against a model that was never correlated outside the box. The trigger such a run fires is the first of §21.4's expiry triggers, a new operating condition, and §21.5's practice of randomizing a nominal supply deliberately beyond its legal band is a run of precisely that kind.

**Solution 21.3.** The AMS boundary can discharge the algorithm and cannot discharge the distribution. §21.3 enumerates the algorithmic obligations it does discharge: that the training state machine converges against a channel model, that it terminates rather than loops when a step fails, that its retry and fallback paths are reachable and correct, and that it respects the protocol's timing obligations throughout, with real models plus a few co-simulated runs covering that substantial algorithmic risk. What it cannot discharge is the obligation Chapter 20 names, the distribution of training outcomes across parts and temperature, and the platform where that one closes is the lab, on parts. The reason §21.3 gives is a property shared by every pre-silicon channel model: each trains against an idealization of signal integrity, so the spread that separates a part converging at the center of its acceptance window from one converging at the edge does not exist until there are parts. The consequence for the plan is that one row becomes two, since §21.3 requires the plan to carry both with the second closing in the lab, and requires that division to be documented before bring-up.

**Solution 21.4.** The two runtimes give 92.5 / 0.4 = 231.25, the ratio §21.2's table rounds to 230× for that row. One hour of real-model regression rerun with the analog solver in the loop therefore costs 1 × 231.25 = 231.25 hours, which is over nine days, and that is §21.3's own reading of the figure: at anything like that ratio an hour of real-model regression takes multiple days with analog solvers in the loop, and a job required overnight misses its deadline. The conclusion §21.3 draws is about scheduling and not about tuning, since it states that the runtime gap cannot be resolved by tuning: AMS co-simulation requires a campaign, a purpose-built run with a stated reason and a written result that then stops, rather than a regression tier with a nightly budget. The reason §21.2 gives against reusing the ratio is that each of its three measurements is against its own baseline, in a different design and a different year, the first row against a SPICE netlist and the other two against Verilog-AMS models of the same blocks; the three ratios measure separate things, so multiplying them would produce an unsupported composite claim, and every ratio must retain its baseline.

**Solution 21.5.** The cause is the shape of the bin rather than the volume of stimulus. §21.1 states that a coverage bin on a real value naming a single point can fail to be hit at all because floating-point representation has limited precision, and it gives the illustration: 0.1 + 0.2 yields 0.30000000000000004, which does not fall in a bin covering {0.3}, and the language standard names this explicitly as a problem for analog specifications that carry tolerance ranges. The remedy §21.1 takes from the same standard is a tolerance token attached to a single real value, which defines an implicit range so that a real measurement can land in the bin. The package of §21.5 writes the bin as bins midscale = {[V_MIDSCALE +/- V_BIN_TOL]}, using the absolute token, and the property that keeps it one bin is the one §21.5 states: its total width is smaller than the real interval, so a measurement near half-scale lands in it instead of falling between two points floating-point arithmetic will never produce exactly. Further runs cannot close the row because no additional sample can be exactly equal to a value the representation does not produce, so what has to change is the declaration and not the seed count, and the report's own reading of the span bins confirms the stimulus reached the region already.

## Chapter 22

**Solution 22.1.** §22.3 reports the chain in one pass: the list starts from 3,606 faults, collapsing removes 973 and testability analysis another 144, leaving 2,489; structural cone-of-influence analysis marks 763 of those unobservable, leaving 1,726; formal observability analysis then clears a further 248, leaving 1,478 faults that reach the read-data port. The reduction therefore removed 3,606 - 1,478 = 2,128 faults in total. Of that total, collapsing accounts for 973, the group §22.3 characterizes as dropped because an equivalent prime fault stays in the list to represent them. The remaining 144 + 763 + 248 = 1,155 belong to the second group, since §22.3 states the dichotomy exhaustively: a collapsed fault has a representative, and every other fault is dropped by an argument that holds under any stimulus whatsoever. The two groups reconcile with the total, 973 + 1,155 = 2,128. §22.3 gives the reason for the order directly: it reduces cost by applying the cheap arguments first, so the expensive engine sees only what the cheaper methods could not settle.

**Solution 22.2.** The four classes §22.3 reports are 225 neither observed nor detected, 23 detected without being observed, 966 both observed and detected, and 512 observed but not detected. The two classes holding the observed faults sum to 966 + 512 = 1,478, the same count the reduction left reaching the read-data port, which is the arithmetic check that the classification and the reduction describe one campaign. The classification's own total is 225 + 23 + 966 + 512 = 1,726, the faults §22.3 says entered detection analysis, so the difference is 1,726 - 1,478 = 248, and that matches the count formal observability analysis cleared. Equal totals are not equal sets, and §22.3 forbids reading the identity as a safety verdict: a row marked not observed has two explanations its results cannot distinguish, either that the fault is safe and cannot affect the safety goal under any stimulus, or that it is dangerous and the workload failed to excite it. Before a fault left unobserved may be classified as safe, §22.3 therefore requires a structural or formal argument that it cannot violate the safety goal under any stimulus; short of that the fault is unresolved and must be resolved through better stimulus, proof, or a recorded engineering judgment with a named owner.

**Solution 22.3.** The FMEDA can do nothing with that percentage: §22.2 states that a single aggregate percentage across a whole block cannot be composed with anything. The property that makes it unusable is that architectural metrics are ratios of failure rates rather than of fault counts. Each failure mode of each design element carries a base failure rate computed from design data and technology data, and the metric formulas combine those rates through the safe, residual and multi-point fractions into a single number for the element and then for the part, so the campaign's detected fraction is an input applied to a weighted quantity and is not itself that quantity. §22.2 requires instead that the fault list be agreed with the safety engineer per failure mode and that results be kept separate by mode through reporting, which is what lets the FMEDA apply the base failure rates. Two campaigns with identical fault counts arrive at different metrics when one list oversamples a large but low-rate structure while undersampling a small critical one: that bias appears in no coverage report, because the report counts faults while the metric weighs rates.

**Solution 22.4.** The fault outside the cone of influence of every observation point may be removed. §22.3 separates the two on what their evidence quantifies over: structural and formal arguments answer under any stimulus, and a fault outside the cone of influence of every observation point cannot reach an observation point under any workload whatsoever, which is a statically established property of the netlist and scales from block to system; simulation answers only under this stimulus, and a workload that fails to excite a fault provides no evidence about its safety. The second fault therefore stays in the list as unresolved, and §22.3 leaves it three dispositions: better stimulus, a proof that it can never cause a difference at the observation point, or a recorded engineering judgment with a named owner. Recording it as safe instead is the inflation §22.3 warns against, since classifying every unexcited fault as safe can raise the reported metrics arbitrarily, and an assessor requires an argument for that classification.

**Solution 22.5.** The empty field is the FMEDA failure mode, and §22.5 makes it the link that keeps the plan row and the failure analysis from drifting apart: the two are maintained by different people in different tools, so each plan row should carry its failure-mode identifier, and without it the row sits off the chain that runs from the vehicle-level hazard through the safety requirement, the failure modes and the mechanism to the specific runs, the archived results and the report that quotes them. The criterion tests only the first of §22.4's three obligations, detection when required, and leaves the other two untested. The first of those is detection within the required interval, which §22.4 makes part of the requirement because a mechanism that reports an error long after the corrupted data has been consumed has not provided useful detection; the second is silence during healthy operation, and it is the one §22.4 discharges with a cross. The stimulus that cross requires is every legal power and clock transition of the watched domain crossed against the monitor's alarm, with the alarm asserted as an error, because the domain legitimately changes frequency during a low-power transition, legitimately stops during power-down and legitimately restarts with a ramp, and every one of those healthy events looks exactly like the failure the monitor exists to catch.

The check §23.5 leaves to a verification engineer is to examine which side of the trust boundary contains the selector's enable, and §23.5 says that examination needs no analysis of the cryptography and none of power analysis.

**Solution 23.4.** The empty part is the activation condition, which §23.4's table describes as when tracking starts and gives, for this engine, as from the cycle a key is loaded rather than from reset. Of the two exception entries, §23.4 licenses the cipher core's ciphertext output as an argument, because ciphertext depends on the key and without that exception the rule fails immediately and correctly, so the flow it permits can be argued as declassification of a trusted transformation; the engine's busy flag records an unresolved defect instead, and §23.4 names that pattern in as many words, a signal added to the ignore list because the rule kept failing. **Solution 25.5.** §25.3 denies the inference because a generator's errors are not distributed the way a human author's are. A generator reproduces one misreading of one recurring pattern identically in every property derived from it, so a sample of five either catches the entire class or misses the entire class and yields no estimate of an error rate at all. The property that decides the point is that correlated errors make sampling uninformative, and §25.4 turns it into a rule of the flow: a review covers every artifact in a batch or rejects the batch. The two unmet admission conditions belong to the second and third human gates.

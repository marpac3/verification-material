Why Verification Exists

The problem, the numbers, the cost — and the design we will keep breaking for the rest of the book.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE3

Chapter 1 · The opening scenario

Eleven days before tape-out, one seed fails

A 2D transfer, negative stride, row crossing a 4 KB boundary, mis-split by the DMA's legalizer — only while the second channel competes for the bus. 214 directed tests had passed for months.

Universe A — the regression caught it

  • Overnight scoreboard mismatch
  • Every internal signal observable
  • The fix takes a day

Universe B — it shipped

  • Silicon boots; bring-up sees nothing for weeks
  • A customer corrupts a buffer, intermittently, under load
  • Weeks of senior time, at a fraction of simulation's visibility
  • Respin, requalification, missed launch window

The whole book in one lineThe difference between these two universes is one nightly regression. Multiplied across every block of every chip, that difference is why verification consumes most of a project.

SayAsk the room which universe their last project was in — and how they know.

Chapter 1 · The other half of the industry

FPGAs: the illusion of a free respin

No mask set, so a fix is "just" a rebuild. Asked in 2024 how many non-trivial bugs escaped into production, only 13% of FPGA projects answered zero.

202487% of FPGA projects shipped production systems with non-trivial bugs — notably worse than the IC/ASIC first-silicon rate.

Why the comfort is false

  • Cultural diagnosis: teams minimise pre-lab verification and race to the bench — a strategy that worked for simple parts, not for SoC-class devices
  • Reprogramming is not free: in aerospace it means removing covers and a full system revalidation
  • The transition is under way: FPGA verification headcount grew 8.5% CAGR against 4.7% for design, 2012–2024

The rule that followsSpend pre-lab effort where the design is new. Reuse of verified IP is what lets some projects legitimately spend less.

Chapter 1 · The effort

"70% of the effort" — folklore, and what was measured

The folklore

  • Quoted in nearly every talk and textbook
  • Also called "subjective or unsubstantiated" — repeated publication to publication without primary data
  • Yet well rooted: ~70% of design effort in the SoC era; ~70% of design time on multiple projects; validation ~70% of SoC time and resources
The defensible claimMore than half — and how much more is set by how much of your design is new.

The measurement

57% of total project time spent in verification, measured in 2014, essentially unchanged from 2012.

  • Wide dispersion in both directions — and a growing tail above 80%
  • Low end: projects assembling pre-verified IP
  • High end: projects with high proportions of newly developed IP

The dispersion is the insight. The 70% figure is not wrong so much as unqualified.

SayThis is a lesson in reading industry numbers: a figure everyone repeats, a figure someone measured, and the honest sentence that survives both.

Chapter 1 · The economics

The cost curve is an observability curve

Cost to find and fix rises; time available shrinks; and behind both sits how much internal state you can see when something goes wrong.

Block RTL simulation — any internal signal, any time  ·  fix = edit and recompile
Full-chip simulation / emulation — reduced  ·  fix = rebuild, hours
Post-silicon bring-up — scan and trace only  ·  workaround or respin
Production / field — near zero  ·  recall, errata, respin
  • Simulation is slow but transparent: roughly a billion times slower than the target silicon — booting an operating system would take years
  • Post-silicon debug is measured in weeks: on one heavily instrumented program, medium-severity bugs took ~10 days to root-cause, 90th percentile 20 days — per bug
  • The industry pre-pays in silicon: in some modern SoCs over 20% of die area is design-for-debug instrumentation
  • The market window dwarfs everything: missing a launch window is documented in the range of millions to billions
  • The "10× per stage" rule is a mnemonic, not data — but no mechanism pushes the other way

Row three, from a reported case: Broadcom reused the existing regression for a “silicon-proven” in-house audio IP whose only change was one added channel. Every RTL regression passed before tape-out; the bug appeared months into silicon testing, and it was closed with a software workaround rather than a re-spin.

Chapter 1 · Worked example

Anatomy of a near-escape

Bug ID:          SOC-1284
Title:           DMA ch0 2D negative-stride transfer corrupts data at 4 KB
                 boundary when ch1 is concurrently active
Found by:        Nightly constrained-random regression (scoreboard mismatch),
                 11 days before tape-out
Severity:        Critical - silent data corruption, no error response raised
Root cause:      Midend transfer legalizer mis-splits a negative-stride 2D row
                 that crosses a 4 KB AXI boundary; the malformed burst is
                 masked unless ch1 owns the backend in the same window.
Escape analysis: 214 directed DMA tests passed; none combined negative stride
                 x 4 KB crossing x dual-channel activity. The functional
                 coverage model had no cross of these three dimensions -
                 the hole was invisible to our own metrics.
Cost if escaped: intermittent field corruption -> weeks of bring-up debug at
                 ~10 days/bug post-silicon norms, likely respin + requal
Actions:         (1) fix legalizer split logic; (2) add coverage cross
                 stride_sign x boundary_cross x channel_concurrency; (3) extend
                 scoreboard with end-to-end check for 2D descriptors; (4) audit
                 all legalizer corner cases against the AXI 4 KB rule.

Note what found it and what did notThe directed tests all passed, honestly. Constrained-random found the unimagined combination; the scoreboard, not any human, noticed the corruption; and the coverage model could not even represent the hole.

SayRead the "cost if escaped" line aloud. A bug report is an economic document.

The Verifier's Mindset

From "make it work" to "prove it does not break" — a different intellectual act, not a second pass over the same one.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE14

Chapter 2 · The opening scenario

Two minds, one specification

The DMA designer verifies her own block in one week — register defaults, 1D and 2D transfers, a bad address, completion interrupts. All pass. Three months later a random seed finds the negative-stride bug her tests could never have reached.

The designerConstructs a component that correctly implements the intended function, with the required performance and, ideally, without defects.
The verifierStarts from the opposite premise — the bugs are already in there — and finds them by stressing protocols, exploring corner cases, and applying zero tolerance toward inconsistencies.
  • Two orientations toward the same specification — not two intensities of one activity.
  • Not a distinction between job titles: designers already spend 49% of their time verifying (2024).
  • Hiring filters on languages and tools. Mindset is the stronger determinant — and takes years, not months.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE16

Chapter 2 · Thinking in terms of breakage

Stress it — do not interoperate with it

The paradox of robustnessBuild a monitor for a protocol needing clock-data recovery. The more robust its recovery algorithm, the less checking it performs — a tolerant monitor gracefully adapts to data rates that are out of spec, masking the bug it exists to catch. The right answer is the least tolerant observer the reference clock permits, even though no real receiver would ever be built that way.

Verification's aim is not to replicate reality but to verify the design in the most thorough manner possible — "even if it means doing so in spite of reality."

The helpful testbenchSymptomCure
HandshakesAuto-NACKs a corrupted transfer, so a simulation that should have failed keeps runningLog an error and fail; NACK generation only under explicit error-injection control
Status outputsTests pace themselves on the FIFO's full/empty flags — the blind leading the blindCheck the flags first (white-box assertion or independent count), then rely on them
Error recoveryRecovery paths keep a failing simulation alive; downstream checking is compromised anywayStop on first error — a mousetrap that has caught its bug is done for that simulation

Source: Montesano & Litterick (Verilab), “Verification Mind Games: how to think like a verifier”, DVCon — which frames the mindset as a series of concrete choices rather than an attitude, the first being whether the environment's duty is to replicate the real world, and names zero tolerance for design inconsistencies as the distinguishing commitment.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE17

Chapter 2 · Corner cases

"Invalid" is not a stimulus filter

Whether a scenario is valid or invalid is irrelevant. The only question is whether it can happen — and if it can, the design's response to it must be verified.

Reported case 1A designer dismissed a zero-radius circle as meaningless input. In that very production system, upstream software was generating zero-radius circles routinely — unknown to him.
Reported case 2A low-power mode bit was tested the obvious way: write 1, check entry; write 0, check exit. It passed until tape-out. Writing 0 to a bit that was already 0 put the device into low-power mode.

On the DMA

  • "Negative stride" is on the list not because anyone plans to use it — but because the register field is signed, so it can happen
  • The verifier crosses the breaking points: stride sign × boundary proximity × channel concurrency × burst length
  • That cross is exactly the cell where the legalizer's bug lives
The reflex to unlearnNothing in the specification suggests either test above. Both bugs are invisible to a mind that tests what the design is for, and near-inevitable finds for a mind that enumerates what the design can be subjected to.
A HOLISTIC GUIDE18

Chapter 2 · Worked example

Attacking the transfer legalizer

The designer thinks "the legalizer splits at 4 KB." The verifier states the prohibition at the output and lets the tool hunt for any way to violate it.

// No INCR write burst emitted by the DMA backend may cross a 4 KB page,
// regardless of how the midend legalized the transfer. (FIXED bursts hold
// their address; WRAP bursts stay inside their aligned container.)
// 16-bit arithmetic on purpose: an illegal awsize (>3 on this 64-bit bus)
// must overflow the comparison and fail - not wrap silently and pass.
property p_no_4kb_crossing;
  @(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n)
  (awvalid && awready && (awburst == 2'b01)) |->
    (16'(awaddr[11:0]) + ((16'(awlen) + 16'd1) << awsize)) <= 16'd4096;
endproperty
a_no_4kb_crossing : assert property (p_no_4kb_crossing);
The propertyThe zero-tolerance check: one rule, every cycle, every test.
The crossThe map of where to apply pressure: stride sign × distance to boundary × burst length × other channel active — a block of empty bins would have exposed the directed suite's blind spot long before any failure.

SayThe 16-bit arithmetic is doubt applied to the verifier's own code — so an illegal size fails loudly instead of wrapping past the check and passing.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE19

Chapter 2 · Judgment

Independence of judgment, and the value of doubt

Three disciplines that protect independence

  • Predict from the specification, not from the design. The moment your checker is tuned until the design passes, you are verifying the design against itself.
  • Trust no signal you have not checked. Every unchecked DUT output delegates a piece of your judgment back to the object under suspicion.
  • Speak as the third party in the room. The verifier is the natural liaison between architect and designer — often the first to detect that the two have diverged.
"Software will never do that"The operative question is not intent but reachability: can the programming interface express it? If yes, either the RTL is fixed or the case is detected and rejected — and a test proves the rejection.

Four doubts

  • Doubt the green. No coverage without checking. A bin hit while nothing verified the outcome is worse than an empty bin — it converts ignorance into confidence.
  • Doubt the quiet. A block that has never failed a test is either simple or under-attacked. Ask which, and reallocate.
  • Doubt your own tools. Much of a testbench's behaviour never appears in waveforms; if you cannot interrogate it from the logs, you have no grounds to trust its verdicts.
  • Doubt the schedule's promises. Success undefined is unfalsifiable. Doubt, written down and agreed before the pressure arrives, is called a plan.

The Verification Problem

Why completeness is not merely expensive but impossible — and what the discipline does about it.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE22

Chapter 3 · The arithmetic that ends the argument

"Test all the cases." Let us count them.

One channel of the reference SoC's DMA engine — the block a feature list would reasonably call simple:

RegisterBitsRole
SRC_ADDR · DST_ADDR32 + 32base addresses
NUM_BYTES32bytes per row
SRC_STRIDE · DST_STRIDE32 + 32signed 2D strides
NUM_REPS162D row count
CFG · STATUS8 + 8flags, error code

192 bits of architecturally visible state → 2192 ≈ 6.3 × 1057 configurations, for one channel.
Now put a number on "visiting" them 1 million new configurations per second × a farm of 1,000 simulations = 109/s.
The universe is ~4.4 × 1017 seconds old.
Running since the Big Bang, that farm reaches 4.4 × 1026 configurations — a fraction of the space around 10−31.
Not one percent. Not one billionth. A denominator of thirty-two digits.

And the register file is the shallow part

  • The two address registers alone: 264 ≈ 1.8 × 1019 pairs — nearly six centuries on that farm
  • One 16 × 64-bit backend FIFO: 1,024 bits → 21024 ≈ 10308
  • Two channels: 2384 ≈ 3.9 × 10115 — where the canonical DMA bug lives
  • Sequences too: ten binary environment choices per cycle, a thousand cycles → one path out of 210000

SayNot a matter of budget: enumeration is off the table before the project starts — and 512 KB of SRAM alone holds over four million state bits.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE23

Chapter 3 · The first honest response

Abstraction shrinks the space — and it is still too big

Most states are equivalent. So partition each dimension into equivalence classes and cross classes instead of bits — for one DMA channel:

DimensionClasses
source offset in 4 KB page~16
destination offset in page~16
length (sub-beat … crosses 2+ pages)~10
stride (sign × relation to row, zero)~6
repetitions (1, 2, many, max)~4
other channel (idle / active / erroring)~4
backend back-pressure (ready delay 0–7)~8
error response (none / read / write / both)~4

The product is about nine million cases — no longer cosmological, still far beyond directed testing. A team maintaining a thousand hand-crafted tests is exceptional.
The actual structure of simulation-based verification1. Abstraction  2. Stimulus automation  3. Measurement.
Name the bargain exactly: relative completeness comes from a coverage metric, and defining that metric is "a selective, and thus subjective, process."

Doesn't formal verification solve this?

  • Partly, and importantly: a proven property holds for every reachable state and input sequence.
  • But model checking is exhaustive state-space search, worst-case exponential in storage elements — the same wall in formal clothes.
  • So full proofs go to small or well-abstracted targets: arbitration, protocols, control kernels.
  • Bounded proofs are honest: guaranteed only within the bound.
  • Rigorous complete verification works — purchasable for selected modules, never ambient.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE25

Chapter 3 · The two classical limits

Controllability and observability

ControllabilityYour ability to steer the design into a given internal condition, using only the interfaces you can drive.
ObservabilityYour ability to see the consequences of an internal event, at the interfaces you can watch.

Smaller targets offer more of both. Verify each feature at a level where you can provoke it and see it fail.

Both collapse on the way to silicon

PlatformWhat you can observe
RTL simulationVirtually any internal signal, at any time
FPGA prototypeA few thousand signals, pre-selected before the bitstream is built
SiliconOrder of a hundred signals routed to trace buffers and pins; control limited to hooks defined in advance

Change the design, don't be a hero

  • Observability problem → sample points readable through memory-mapped registers
  • Controllability problem → provisions to force error and exception conditions
  • Assertions do it from inside: the error is flagged at its birthplace

SayThe budget you enjoy in simulation is the best you will ever have. Every bug missed here must be caught somewhere it is ten times harder to see.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE26

Chapter 3 · The hardest link

The oracle problem: how do you know the response is right?

A simulator produces what the design did. Correctness relates that to what it should have done — and "should" lives in prose, in the architect's head, or nowhere. Every practical oracle is partial.

OracleWhat it checksWhat it is structurally blind to
Reference modelAn independent executable of the specification, compared continuously — golden by definition for processorsAnything outside its projection: performance, protocol legality, timing side channels
ScoreboardData integrity and completeness of a transform: nothing lost, nothing inventedWhen things happened (latency, fairness, starvation) and how (a protocol violation delivering correct data)
AssertionsLocal invariants and protocol obligations, every cycle, on every test — the sharpest partial oracleEverything outside the one rule each assertion states
Offline / humanPost-simulation comparison; some responses are easy for a human, awkward for a programArrives far from the state that caused it — diagnosis is cheap only near the failure

The failure that defeats all oracles at onceThe common-mode error. If the scoreboard author misreads the same ambiguous sentence the designer misread, model and design agree and the bug is structurally invisible. A passing regression says the design and the testbench agree — not that either is right.

A HOLISTIC GUIDE27

Chapter 3 · Worked example

What one assertion proves — and what it does not

Interrogate the 4 KB property from Chapter 2, deliberately, question by question.

QuestionDoes a_no_4kb_crossing answer it?
Did any issued burst cross a 4 KB boundary?YES  Every INCR burst, every cycle, every test — FIXED and WRAP cannot cross by construction
Did the split bursts carry the right data?NO  It never looks at data
Do the split bursts add up to the programmed transfer?NO  It sees bursts one at a time, not their sum
Does a legal transfer ever complete at all?NO  A backend that hangs forever satisfies it vacuously
Was the read channel also legal?NO  Separate assertion needed

The division of labour, on the canonical bugThe emitted bursts were individually legal — the assertion stayed silent. The data landed wrong, and only the scoreboard's byte-accurate memory model caught it. Assertion and scoreboard each covered the other's blind spot.

SayThe question to internalise is not "which oracle is best" but "which projection of correctness is each oracle responsible for, and is any projection unowned?"

A HOLISTIC GUIDE28

Chapter 3 · The intellectual pivot of the book

From exhaustive to risk-driven

Assemble the three results

  • You cannot visit all the states — finite, yet unvisitable
  • You cannot reach or see everything even in the fragment you do visit
  • You cannot fully check even what you see — every oracle is a partial projection of an imperfect specification
Only one conclusion is availableVerification can show the presence of errors, not their absence. Given enough time, an error will be found. So the question becomes:
"Is the error likely to be severe enough to warrant the effort spent identifying it?"

What changes the morning after

  • Effort goes where risk is, not where habit is. Risk = likelihood × escape cost. Likelihood concentrates in novelty and intersections; cost concentrates where escapes are expensive to find downstream.
  • Techniques are matched to the shape of each risk. Random simulation covers the reachable majority fast; directed tests reach a known corner precisely at engineer-days each; formal is exhaustive per property where it converges.
  • "Done" is redefined. Not "verified everything" but "reached the agreed evidence threshold on every planned item, and the risks we chose not to retire are enumerated and accepted by people entitled to accept them."

You are never finished. You stop, on purpose, in the open.

SayTeams that pretend otherwise still stop — the tape-out date sees to that — but they stop with their residual risk unexamined.

One lifecycle per levelThe crossbar runs its own complete cycle at block level; the SoC-level cycle consumes its results and gates its own bring-up on the crossbar reaching maturity. Levels exist to put each class of bug where it is cheapest to find.

How far PLAN has to reach: for the IBM POWER8, bring-up tooling was specified at high-level design, so hardware irritators, non-functional stress modes and embedded checkers were designed into the chip for validation — and IBM counted any bug first found by an OS-based test or by real software as an escape from its validation strategy (IEEE Design & Test, 2017).

A HOLISTIC GUIDE32

Chapter 4 · Managing it

Milestones, not percentages

Uncheckable"Agent coding 80% done."
"We're running lots of tests."
"Soon."
Either true or false"A single packet goes through the DUT and the scoreboard checks it."
"The register reset-value test passes in regression."

Lifecycle milestones: plan review · first transaction · feature complete · RTL freeze · coverage closure · verification sign-off. The vocabulary varies; the pattern does not.

Planning is the highest-leverage phase

  • Verification effort runs 100% to 200% of the RTL design effort. No team would spend the design half without a specification — the verification plan is that specification.
  • Audit your reuse: for every piece of collateral, is it reused / modified / new? Assuming "reuse" means 100% reuse is how projects arrive at bring-up under-scoped.
  • Plan in deliverables with a definition of done that is an action, not a state.
  • Reported ROI (one organization's figures): 10–20% added to the verification front end, returning 10–40% of the overall schedule and at least 20% of implementation and closure effort.

SayTreat the ROI numbers as one team's experience. The direction — planning is the highest-return phase — is consistent across every source in the chapter.

Chapter 4 · A shared language

The maturity ladder — and a stage exit in the open

Stage 3 — Verified / sign-off ready
Coverage closed against the plan, every exclusion reviewed; regression passing over a sustained window; every open bug dispositioned; the sign-off review has accepted the evidence.
Stage 2 — Verification mature
All planned stimulus and checkers implemented; coverage model reporting; regression at cadence with a managed pass rate; bug-open curve past peak. The gate others build on.
Stage 1 — Bring-up complete
Plan reviewed; the environment drives real transactions against current RTL and checks them automatically; a smoke suite passes in regression.

Each line is objectively true or false, so a stage claim is auditable in an hour by an outsider — and the stages compose upward into an SoC dashboard.

The crossbar's stage-2 exit checklist, week 16

[x] Verification plan reviewed; actions closed
[x] All 5x4 manager/subordinate paths driven and checked
[x] Protocol assertions bound on all 9 ports; sim + formal
[x] Coverage model XB-01..XB-07; reporting nightly
[x] Nightly regression >= 300 seeds, pass >= 95%
[x] Bug-open curve past peak (closure > discovery)
[ ] Formal ordering proofs (XB-04): 2 still bounded-only
[ ] Starvation metric (XB-05) not yet on dashboard

Two open boxes: the crossbar has not exited stage 2 — and everyone sees exactly why.

The warningStages describe verification maturity, not RTL quality. A block can be stage 2 with fifty open bugs: the claim is that the machinery to find them is mature.

Chapter 4 · Schedule reality

The crunch, the guillotine, and the gap you created

What effort actually does

  • Planning and build spend at a modest, steady rate; the long middle burns compute and debug time
  • Closure concentrates the highest-pressure work — last coverage holes, intermittent failures, hardest bugs — into the weeks when the date is least movable. That is the crunch.
The guillotineWhen a fixed ship date simply cuts off whatever verification was still in flight, what ships is "whatever happened to work" — and the features in the basket may be the ones the market needed.

The defence was built in week one: a prioritised plan converts the guillotine into a negotiation, and a signature on the risk register converts "we ran out of time" into an accountable decision.

The verification gap, decomposed

ClassWhat to do about it
InherentComplexity you must absorb — innovate
TransientSkills and tooling that lag but catch up — invest
Self-inducedSchedule pressure forcing shortcuts; best-known methods unused (estimated ~20% productivity cost); sloppy environment engineering — introspect

Individual productivity spread across engineers is reported up to 10×, which makes team composition a first-order schedule variable no methodology hides.

Much of this book aims at the third class: the gap you created and can close without waiting for anyone's roadmap.

Recall the ambient numbers: 14% first-silicon success for IC/ASIC, and 75% of IC/ASIC projects behind schedule in 2024 — up from a historical ~66% (Wilson Research Group 2024, pp. 5–6); 87% of FPGA projects shipping non-trivial escapes into production (Wilson Research Group 2024, FPGA study). The ~20% best-known-methods cost and the up-to-10× productivity spread above are Oracle Labs' own printed bullets — I created the Verification Gap, DVCon U.S. 2015, pp. 7, 9.

Part I · Closing

What Part I established

  • Verification is economics. Bug cost rises and fix time shrinks at every stage.
  • Mindset, not tools, determines effectiveness. Verifying your own design reconverges on your interpretation, not the specification.
  • Completeness is not on offer. The space is unvisitable, observability is bounded, every oracle is partial.
  • So "done" is a risk-acceptance decision, made in the open — which is what the lifecycle exists to produce.
The pivot to carry forwardReplace "have we verified everything?" (always no) with "is the residual risk known, bounded, and accepted?"
